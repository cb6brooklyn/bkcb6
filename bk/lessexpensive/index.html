Housing and its price are often discussed, directly and indirectly. In every corner of New York City, even more so. Those discussions can be indirect, like with the countless articles pondering “why are so many young adults living with their parents?” I haven’t read all of them, but the bottom line is it’s due to housing costs.

This page aims to simplify and clarify what housing costs are based on what’s available at a given time. I used all listings from 2010 to today based on StreetEasy.com’s listings. I broke it down from micro (cb6/ neighborhoods) to macro (borough and citywide).

Rest assured, it’s not just staring at numbers that I’d say are too high; it’s also got every active “affordable housing” lottery open across NYC. I put “affordable” in quotes, as that may be better described as “less expensive housing”, but I digress. I’ll keep making updates, but check it out and let me know what you think.

How affordable housing works here

Affordable means income regulated. Rent is capped and tied to a share of Area Median Income, and you qualify by earning within a set range, with both a floor and a ceiling.

Applying is free and happens through NYC Housing Connect. Nobody should ever charge you to apply. Each lottery has its own deadline and its own income and household size requirements.

The eligibility tool is a guide, not a decision. The listing itself is the authority on who qualifies.

How eligibility is decided
Only two things decide eligibility: headcount and total counted income. Who earns it does not matter. Two adults earning $30,000 each and one adult earning $60,000 with a baby are the same household to HPD: 2 people, $60,000. Both qualify from the same AMI band. But that same $60,000 with only one person in the household qualifies from a higher band, because the limits rise with household size.

Everyone who will live in the unit counts toward headcount, including infants, children, and dependents with no income. Income counted is generally the gross (pre-tax) income of household members 18 and over.

Unit size follows occupancy standards. Every open lottery uses the same ranges: studio 1–2 people, 1-bedroom 1–3, 2-bedroom 2–5, 3-bedroom 3–7, 4-bedroom 4–9. These are the household-size ranges printed in each lottery's own Available Units table on Housing Connect. They set who may apply for a size, not who must: a household of three can apply for a 1-bedroom, 2-bedroom or 3-bedroom. HPD sets these in its Marketing Handbook and revised them upward by one person per unit size, so older published ranges may differ.

AMI is set each year by the federal government (HUD) for the whole New York metro area, so it can be higher than the actual median in many neighborhoods. Figures here are the NYC HPD 2026 chart. A household usually must fall between a unit's minimum and maximum income, so earning too little can also make you ineligible for a given unit.

Free help near you
Neighborhood Preservation Companies

These are nonprofits contracted by New York State to provide housing services in a defined area. Not city agencies, not landlords. Most offer tenant rights counselling, help with repairs and code complaints, eviction and foreclosure prevention, and help applying for affordable housing. Services are generally free.

NYS DHCR & Neighborhood Preservation

Free housing help, close to home, in every borough

New York State Homes and Community Renewal, known as HCR or DHCR, is the State's housing agency. It supervises rent regulation, Mitchell-Lama, and the money that finances affordable housing across the state. Two of the things it does show up directly in your neighborhood.

Neighborhood Preservation Companies are nonprofits DHCR contracts to provide housing services in a specific service area, sometimes called a catchment area. Most offer some combination of managing affordable housing, tenant rights counseling, help with repairs and code complaints, foreclosure and eviction prevention, help applying for affordable housing, and assistance for landlords of small buildings.

Services are generally free. Each company serves a (sort of) defined area. If you are reading this in CB6, the one closest to you is probably either the Fifth Avenue Committee or the Carroll Gardens Association.

How to apply
Applying through NYC Housing Connect
Housing Connect is the city's official portal for affordable housing lotteries, run by HPD and HDC. It is free to apply.
  1. Create a free profile at Housing Connect with your household size and income.
  2. Search open lotteries and filter to units that match your household and income band.
  3. Apply to any you qualify for. Applying to multiple lotteries is allowed and encouraged.
  4. If your application is selected, you will be asked to verify income and household documents.
  5. Never pay a fee to apply. The lottery and application are always free.
The other lottery
Mitchell-Lama and State housing
Housing Connect is the city's list. New York State runs its own, and the two do not overlap. If you only check one, you are missing half of what is open.

Mitchell-Lama is middle income housing, both rentals and co-ops. Created in 1955, the developments are privately owned but income regulated, and many have income ceilings well above the city lotteries. A one person household can qualify up to roughly $141,750 in a non federally assisted development, and a four person household up to roughly $202,500.

Most Mitchell-Lama openings are waitlists, not lotteries. You apply to each development separately, and a place on the list can take years to move. Some lists are open, some are closed. Veterans may be entitled to a preference at developments with an open list.

It all moved to one site in 2025. NYS Housing Search replaced the old Mitchell-Lama Automated Waiting List, and now carries every HCR funded listing, lottery and waitlist in one place. If you applied before May 2026, your existing applications can be linked to a new account.

  1. Create a free account at NYS Housing Search.
  2. Filter by household size, income, county and bedrooms, and tick Mitchell-Lama to see only those.
  3. Apply to every development you qualify for. Each has its own list.
  4. Check your position and update your contact details through the same account.
  5. Never pay to apply. It is free, the same as Housing Connect.

Where New York State put its housing dollars in New York City
New York State publishes every project it funds under its housing plans, but only as a map of dots and totals by county, senate and assembly district. None of those lines match how the city is actually governed. This takes the same projects and puts them where New Yorkers can use them: by community district, by council district, and by neighborhood.
