The story so far

The city has been trying to fix the oldest stretch of the BQE for most of a decade. Three mayors have now put out plans for it: Bill de Blasio, Eric Adams and, this August, Zohran Mamdani.

What people may not know

3 facts
The triple cantilever is a stacked highway. For 0.4 miles, BQE Central is "two levels of highway with the Brooklyn Heights Promenade above and a local street, Furman Street, below" (NYC DOT).
It is past its design life. DOT says the structure is "more than 70 years old" (NYC DOT).
Why it matters in CB6. Limits on BQE Central, fewer lanes and weight enforcement, can push traffic off the highway and onto local streets. The rest of this page is what the public record shows about trucks on these streets.

Who controls which part

City, state and federal
CityBQE Central

Owns BQE Central, about 1.5 miles from Atlantic Avenue to Sands Street, and runs this project.

More

About 1.5 miles, Atlantic Avenue to Sands Street, with the triple cantilever. "NYC DOT owns BQE Central (12% of the BQE in Brooklyn)" and leads this project and its city environmental review, CEQR (NYC DOT). The two lanes and the weight sensors on BQE Central are the city's (amNY). The city also sets the truck routes on local streets (NYC DOT, Truck Routing).

StateBQE North and BQE South

Owns the other 10.6 miles in Brooklyn: BQE North and BQE South.

More

"New York State owns the rest," the other 10.6 miles in Brooklyn (NYC DOT): BQE North, "from the Kosciuszko Bridge to Sands Street," and BQE South, "from Atlantic Avenue to the Verrazzano Bridge" (BQE Corridor Vision), which in CB6 includes the below-ground stretch Streetsblog calls "the Carroll Gardens trench" (Streetsblog). The city's plan is meant to let it "work with the State, which controls the northern and southern segments" (NYC Mayor's Office).

FederalAll of it, as Interstate 278

The whole BQE is Interstate 278, so changing it needs federal approval.

More

"The BQE is part of the federal interstate highway system," and "If we were to completely remove BQE Central, or alter even a single on- or off-ramp, it would require federal permission," DOT Commissioner Mike Flynn wrote. Among the reasons a bigger change is off the table now, he named "a federal administration with very different priorities" (Streetsblog). The current plan stays inside what the city can do on its own: the interchanges will not be altered, and the project goes through the city's environmental review, CEQR, not a federal one (NYC DOT). The Adams plan had been headed for a federal review (NYC Mayor's Office).

1937

Construction begins

The BQE was "first proposed in the mid-1930s"; work on what was then called the Brooklyn-Queens Connecting Roadway "started in 1937," and the Kosciuszko Bridge opened in 1939 (NYC Parks).

1940

Robert Moses plans the route south

"The Triborough Bridge and Tunnel Authority, under Chairman Robert Moses (1888–1981), detailed a plan in 1940" to extend the road south through Brooklyn (NYC Parks).

1943

The route moves to the waterfront

One plan would have cut through the middle of Brooklyn Heights; opposition from groups like the Brooklyn Heights Association, and budget concerns, led planners "to opt instead for a waterfront route" (Brooklyn Waterfront History).

1946 to 1951

The Promenade

Demolition of the waterfront warehouses began in 1946; the Promenade was built from 1950 to 1951 on top of the stacked highway (Brooklyn Waterfront History).

1954

The cantilever opens

"The cantilevered section of the expressway opened in 1954 at Brooklyn Heights" (NYC Parks).

1958

It becomes a federal highway

"In 1958, the BQE was designated part of the federal highway system, and became known also as Interstate 278" (NYC Parks).

1964

Completed

"By the time the six-lane expressway was completed in 1964, the entire road cost $137 million" (NYC Parks).

November 2016

The first rebuild estimate

DOT put the reconstruction of the 1.5 miles from Atlantic Avenue to Sands Street at $1.7 billion and asked New York State to pay 38 percent (NYC DOT project update).

2018

Two options

DOT offered a "lane-by-lane reconstruction" or a "temporary 6-lane highway on the Promenade," and favored the second; the Brooklyn Heights Association objected to "the loss of the Promenade for six or more years" (Brooklyn Heights Association).

2019

An expert panel

The city convened an expert panel to review the options (NYC DOT).

January 2020

The panel reports

It recommended cutting the highway to two lanes each way (BQE Expert Panel Report, p. 18).

August 2021

De Blasio's 20-year fix

A plan "to extend the life of the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway (BQE) cantilever for at least another 20 years" (NYC DOT); on August 30, 2021 the lanes from Atlantic Avenue to the Brooklyn Bridge went from three to two each way (NYC DOT). The plan also called for "weigh-in-motion" sensors (Streetsblog).

December 2022

Adams re-envisions BQE Central

The Adams administration released "preliminary design concepts for a re-envisioned BQE Central" (NYC Mayor's Office).

February 2023

Three designs

DOT showed "The Terraces, The Lookout, and The Stoop" and announced "a comprehensive traffic study of both two- and three-lane configurations" (NYC DOT). Its feedback maps drew 118 comments on BQE Central and 122 on BQE South.

November 2023

Weight enforcement

Sensors on BQE Central began ticketing overweight trucks (amNY).

October 2024

The state-owned sections

DOT's BQE North and South report proposed "streetscape and intersection redesigns, dedicated bike and bus infrastructure, highway capping, and new plazas" (NYC DOT).

December 2024

BQE Central options

DOT's public meeting listed options from "maintaining the structure" to "removing the highway, replacing it with a boulevard, or tunneling" (NYC DOT); the Central Vision report summarized the public input (NYC DOT).

August 2026

Mamdani: rehabilitate what is there

An approximately $4 billion, 10-year rehabilitation of BQE Central that keeps two lanes each way and breaks ground in 2030 (NYC Mayor's Office). The plan in detail.

September 2026

DOT makes its case

Commissioner Mike Flynn wrote that the plan "isn't ideal" but that "We have years left, not decades" (Streetsblog).

Late 2026 to early 2028

The environmental review

Scoping notice in September 2026, public scoping meetings in November 2026, a final scoping report early in 2027, a draft environmental impact statement with public meetings late in 2027, and the final statement early in 2028 (NYC DOT). This fall's sessions are at the top of the page.

2029 or 2030 to 2040

Construction

DOT expects construction "to begin in 2029 and finish by 2040" (NYC DOT); the Mayor's Office says it will "break ground in 2030" (NYC Mayor's Office).

What this page means by CB6 and its neighborhoods

CB6 is Brooklyn Community District 6, the area Brooklyn Community Board 6 serves (the thick navy line). Its line comes from the bkcb6.app community district boundary file, drawn from NYC Department of City Planning community district boundaries. A number "in CB6" means a record whose location falls inside that line.

Neighborhoods are not official, and people draw them differently. Each neighborhood number uses the outlines on all bkcb6.app maps (city-neighborhoods.geojson), clipped to CB6. The main six are Park Slope, Carroll Gardens, Cobble Hill, Red Hook, Gowanus and the Columbia Street Waterfront District. Some CB6 blocks fall in outlines named Boerum Hill, Greenwood Heights, Prospect Heights, South Slope, and they are counted under those names.

Not inside any outline: the gaps between outlines, including much of the BQE trench and the Atlantic Avenue approach, are counted only in the CB6 total.

Along the BQE: the highway crosses eight community districts, from Bay Ridge to Jackson Heights; comparisons between them use the same district boundary file.

At a glance

Every number links to its source. Each tile says what the number means and what to compare it to; the sections below explain how each was counted.

Two different things. A truck on the BQE is on a highway, not on a CB6 street. The BQE is part of the through truck route network, which trucks crossing Brooklyn without a stop must use. The trucks on CB6 streets are fewer, but they are on the blocks where people live. No public count says how many of the trucks on CB6 streets came off the BQE. This page keeps the two apart: On the BQE numbers are about the highway; On CB6 streets numbers are about the neighborhoods.

How the BQE connects to CB6 streets

The BQE runs through CB6 in the Carroll Gardens trench and along the Gowanus Expressway. Truck routes are the rules for what its trucks may do when they leave the highway.

  • Trucks passing through should stay on the BQE. The BQE and the Gowanus Expressway are through truck routes. A truck with no stop in Brooklyn "must" use through routes (NYC DOT, Truck Routing), so it has no reason to be on a CB6 street.
  • Trucks with a stop in CB6 get off at a ramp. From a ramp, a truck must use local truck routes such as Hamilton Avenue, 3rd Avenue, 4th Avenue and Columbia Street, and leave them only at "the intersection that is nearest to his/her destination, proceed by the most direct route, and then return to the nearest designated truck route using the most direct route" (NYC DOT, Truck Routing). The ramps are on the map.
  • Trucks that dodge BQE limits end up on local streets. BQE Central, just north of CB6, is down to two lanes on the triple cantilever and has weight sensors (NYC DOT; amNY). Streetsblog reported Queens-bound drivers who "get off the highway before the Carroll Gardens trench and cut through Columbia or Hicks streets, before getting back on the BQE at Atlantic Avenue" (Streetsblog, March 20, 2023). DOT counted 15,142 vehicles a day northbound on Hicks Street between Degraw and Kane, against 1,386 southbound; that block is not a truck route (Cobble Hill counts).
  • What is at stake. DOT warns: "Further delay would result in removing trucks or all traffic from the highway and diverting them to local streets, creating unsafe conditions and severely impacting quality-of-life in surrounding neighborhoods" (NYC DOT). If trucks are pushed off the highway, the truck routes on this page are the streets they would be allowed to use.

The city's plan for BQE Central

On August 24, 2026 the Mayor's Office announced "the approximately $4 billion, 10-year rehabilitation will break ground in 2030" for "the City-owned portion of the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway (BQE) between Atlantic Avenue and Sands Street" (NYC Mayor's Office).

  • Why: "The infrastructure is 20 years past its design life and deteriorating under the weight of 130,000 vehicles every day" (NYC Mayor's Office).
  • Bypasses: "a temporary, two-level highway bypass on top of Furman Street, below the promenade, from Atlantic Avenue to Columbia Heights," and "a second temporary, elevated highway bypass from Columbia Heights, over the Brooklyn Bridge approach, to the Manhattan Bridge" (NYC Mayor's Office).
  • Lanes and lifespan: Streetsblog reported that Mamdani and his team "vowed not to increase the BQE's footprint, and to keep it at two lanes in each direction," and that construction would "extend the structure's lifespan to 2080," as city officials told reporters (Streetsblog, August 24, 2026).
  • Cost and funding on DOT's project page: "Rehabilitation is estimated to cost $3.5-$4 billion, a cost that will be refined through the environmental review process," and "The City currently has $1.6 billion allocated to this project in the Capital Plan." The same page says "Construction is expected to begin in 2029 and finish by 2040," a year earlier than the Mayor's Office's 2030 groundbreaking (NYC DOT, BQE Central Project).
  • Environmental review: "The agency expects to complete an environmental impact statement in summer 2028" (NYC Mayor's Office). "During the public scoping period, which will begin later this fall, the public can comment on what the EIS should study" (NYC DOT).

Lanes and weight on the cantilever

DOT says the highway "is currently safe because NYC DOT has made targeted interim repairs and limited stress on the structure by implementing automated enforcement against overweight trucks and reducing traffic to two lanes on the triple cantilever" (NYC DOT, BQE Central Project).

  • Two lanes. The Expert Panel "recommends an immediate reduction from three to two lanes of traffic in each direction to discourage all vehicles but especially trucks" (BQE Expert Panel Report, p. 18 of the PDF). In June 2024 Streetsblog wrote that "former Mayor Bill de Blasio reduced the BQE from three lanes in each direction to two three years ago" (Streetsblog, June 20, 2024).
  • Overweight trucks before enforcement. Data from October 16, 2019 to January 19, 2020 "showed that on the Queens-bound roadway, 11.1 percent of the trucks exceeded 80,000 pounds and 27 percent exceeded the Federal Bridge Formula" (BQE Expert Panel Report, p. 12 of the PDF).
  • Where the sensors are. "The sensors are only deployed on the city-owned section of the BQE between Atlantic Avenue and Sands Street in Brooklyn" (amNY, November 9, 2023). "Beginning November 13, 2023, overweight trucks will be subject to a $650 fine per weight limit violation" (NYC DOT press release).
  • Results Queens-bound. "In the first seven months of enforcement, the agency recorded a monthly average of 2,769 overweight trucks, down from 7,777 in the seven previous months," a 64% drop (Gothamist, July 30, 2024).
  • Staten Island-bound. "Enforcement under the new system began June 22 after a 90-day period that resulted in more than 3,000 notices sent to trucks exceeding state weight limits" (Transport Topics, June 24, 2025).
  • Rerouting is unmeasured. A spokeswoman told FreightWaves, "We are working with the NYPD to enforce this on surrounding streets to prevent trucks from hopping off the BQE and back on again" (FreightWaves, August 1, 2024). ENR wrote that Tanvi Pandya, executive director of DOT's bridge division, "says that from that data alone, it's hard to know if drivers are simply rerouting, as opposed to reducing their weight" (ENR, August 1, 2024).

Enforcement: tickets, crashes and 311 in CB6

NYPD writes tickets to trucks off their routes, under 34 RCNY §4-13, the city truck route rule, and to vehicles that are too big or too heavy, under VTL §385, "Dimensions and weights of vehicles". NYPD publishes each moving violation ticket with where it was written (2018 to 2025, 2026 through June 30). Crashes are NYPD reports where at least one vehicle is a truck type (Motor Vehicle Collisions, July 2012 to June 2026). "On or beside the BQE" means on the BQE, an expressway or a ramp by name, or within about 40 meters of the BQE lines (calculated).

        What the data cannot show, and what to ask DOT for

        The counts show a large one-way flow on northbound Hicks and heavy truck flows on the truck routes. They do not identify which vehicles came off the BQE; none of the sources reviewed here publishes an origin-destination study for these exits.

        • Ramp counts by vehicle class at Hamilton Avenue, Columbia Street, Hicks Street and the Atlantic Avenue on-ramp. The Expert Panel reported for the Atlantic ramp that "approximately 700 to 800 vehicles used the ramp during the AM peak hour and about 500 during the PM peak hour," with no truck split (BQE Expert Panel Report, p. 46 of the PDF).
        • An exit-to-reentry study matching vehicles leaving at Hamilton or Columbia with vehicles reentering at Atlantic, the route Streetsblog described.
        • Both analyses in the BQE Central EIS. DOT says "The EIS will study topics such as construction, traffic, noise, air quality, parks and open space, historic resources, neighborhood character, and other potential effects" (NYC DOT, BQE Central Project).
        • Newer classification counts. In DOT's classification dataset, the latest counts are from 2019 on Columbia Street and 2015 on Hamilton Avenue, and no Henry Street count was found.

        Where the boundaries come from

        • Blocks. The street centerline between two cross streets as the city draws it (NYC Street Centerline); pedestrian paths and highway ramps do not count as cross streets.
        • 100 feet. This page's own choice, not a city definition: it reaches across each intersection and to the building fronts on both sides. Records within 100 feet of two blocks count on the nearer one, so the blocks add up.
        • Districts. From the city's boundary files; a block is assigned to the district its midpoint falls in, and a block a boundary runs through lists both.
