From Moses to Mamdani

Robert Moses planned the BQE's route through Brooklyn, and it was finished in 1964. Its oldest stretch is now past its design life, and multiple mayors have put out plans to fix it.

What people may not know

3 facts
The triple cantilever is a stacked highway. For 0.4 miles, BQE Central is "two levels of highway with the Brooklyn Heights Promenade above and a local street, Furman Street, below" (NYC DOT).
It is past its design life. DOT says the structure is "more than 70 years old" (NYC DOT).
Why it matters in Carroll Gardens and Gowanus. Limits on BQE Central, fewer lanes and weight enforcement, can push traffic off the highway and onto local streets. The rest of this page is what the public record shows about trucks on these streets.

Who controls which part

City, state and federal
CityBQE Central

Owns BQE Central, about 1.5 miles from Atlantic Avenue to Sands Street, and runs this project.

More

About 1.5 miles, Atlantic Avenue to Sands Street, with the triple cantilever. "NYC DOT owns BQE Central (12% of the BQE in Brooklyn)" and leads this project and its city environmental review, CEQR (NYC DOT). The two lanes and the weight sensors on BQE Central are the city's (amNY). The city also sets the truck routes on local streets (NYC DOT, Truck Routing).

StateBQE North and BQE South

Owns the other 10.6 miles in Brooklyn: BQE North and BQE South.

More

"New York State owns the rest," the other 10.6 miles in Brooklyn (NYC DOT): BQE North, "from the Kosciuszko Bridge to Sands Street," and BQE South, "from Atlantic Avenue to the Verrazzano Bridge" (BQE Corridor Vision), which runs past Carroll Gardens and Gowanus. The city's plan is meant to let it "work with the State, which controls the northern and southern segments" (NYC Mayor's Office).

FederalAll of it, as Interstate 278

The whole BQE is Interstate 278, so changing it needs federal approval.

More

"The BQE is part of the federal interstate highway system," and "If we were to completely remove BQE Central, or alter even a single on- or off-ramp, it would require federal permission," DOT Commissioner Mike Flynn wrote. Among the reasons a bigger change is off the table now, he named "a federal administration with very different priorities" (Streetsblog). The current plan stays inside what the city can do on its own: the interchanges will not be altered, and the project goes through the city's environmental review, CEQR, not a federal one (NYC DOT). The Adams plan had been headed for a federal review (NYC Mayor's Office).

1937

Construction begins

The BQE was "first proposed in the mid-1930s"; work on what was then called the Brooklyn-Queens Connecting Roadway "started in 1937," and the Kosciuszko Bridge opened in 1939 (NYC Parks).

1940

Robert Moses plans the route south

"The Triborough Bridge and Tunnel Authority, under Chairman Robert Moses (1888–1981), detailed a plan in 1940" to extend the road south through Brooklyn (NYC Parks).

1943

The route moves to the waterfront

One plan would have cut through the middle of Brooklyn Heights; opposition from groups like the Brooklyn Heights Association, and budget concerns, led planners "to opt instead for a waterfront route" (Brooklyn Waterfront History).

1946 to 1951

The Promenade

Demolition of the waterfront warehouses began in 1946; the Promenade was built from 1950 to 1951 on top of the stacked highway (Brooklyn Waterfront History).

1954

The cantilever opens

"The cantilevered section of the expressway opened in 1954 at Brooklyn Heights" (NYC Parks).

1958

It becomes a federal highway

"In 1958, the BQE was designated part of the federal highway system, and became known also as Interstate 278" (NYC Parks).

1964

Completed

"By the time the six-lane expressway was completed in 1964, the entire road cost $137 million" (NYC Parks).

November 2016

The first rebuild estimate

DOT put the reconstruction of the 1.5 miles from Atlantic Avenue to Sands Street at $1.7 billion and asked New York State to pay 38 percent (NYC DOT project update).

2018

Two options

DOT offered a "lane-by-lane reconstruction" or a "temporary 6-lane highway on the Promenade," and favored the second; the Brooklyn Heights Association objected to "the loss of the Promenade for six or more years" (Brooklyn Heights Association).

2019

An expert panel

The city convened an expert panel to review the options (NYC DOT).

January 2020

The panel reports

It recommended cutting the highway to two lanes each way (BQE Expert Panel Report, p. 18).

August 2021

De Blasio's 20-year fix

A plan "to extend the life of the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway (BQE) cantilever for at least another 20 years" (NYC DOT); on August 30, 2021 the lanes from Atlantic Avenue to the Brooklyn Bridge went from three to two each way (NYC DOT). The plan also called for "weigh-in-motion" sensors (Streetsblog).

December 2022

Adams re-envisions BQE Central

The Adams administration released "preliminary design concepts for a re-envisioned BQE Central" (NYC Mayor's Office).

February 2023

Three designs

DOT showed "The Terraces, The Lookout, and The Stoop" and announced "a comprehensive traffic study of both two- and three-lane configurations" (NYC DOT). Its feedback maps drew 118 comments on BQE Central and 122 on BQE South.

November 2023

Weight enforcement

Sensors on BQE Central began ticketing overweight trucks (amNY).

October 2024

The state-owned sections

DOT's BQE North and South report proposed "streetscape and intersection redesigns, dedicated bike and bus infrastructure, highway capping, and new plazas" (NYC DOT).

December 2024

BQE Central options

DOT's public meeting listed options from "maintaining the structure" to "removing the highway, replacing it with a boulevard, or tunneling" (NYC DOT); the Central Vision report summarized the public input (NYC DOT).

August 2026

Mamdani: rehabilitate what is there

An approximately $4 billion, 10-year rehabilitation of BQE Central that breaks ground in 2030, with two temporary bypasses, one on Furman Street (NYC Mayor's Office). DOT says "The highway will remain two lanes and will not expand, nor will interchanges be altered" (NYC DOT).

September 2026

DOT makes its case

Commissioner Mike Flynn wrote that the plan "isn't ideal" but that "We have years left, not decades" (Streetsblog).

Late 2026 to early 2028

The environmental review

Scoping notice in September 2026, public scoping meetings in November 2026, a final scoping report early in 2027, a draft environmental impact statement with public meetings late in 2027, and the final statement early in 2028 (NYC DOT). This fall's sessions are at the top of the page.

2029 or 2030 to 2040

Construction

DOT expects construction "to begin in 2029 and finish by 2040" (NYC DOT); the Mayor's Office says it will "break ground in 2030" (NYC Mayor's Office).

No public count can say how much BQE traffic ends up on the streets of Carroll Gardens and Gowanus, because no one has tracked vehicles from the exit ramps onto specific blocks. The counts that do exist show two very different pictures. On the BQE itself, DOT counted about 11,729 trucks a day next to Carroll Gardens (between Kane and Sackett Streets, April 2023), out of roughly 13,000 a day on the whole highway. Those trucks are on a through truck route, and a truck with no stop in Brooklyn is required to stay on it.

On neighborhood streets, the numbers are much smaller: the busiest block DOT has counted in Carroll Gardens is Court Street at 351 trucks a day, and in Gowanus it is 3rd Avenue at 594. In Carroll Gardens, none of the four counted blocks is a truck route, so those trucks are either stopping nearby or off route, and the data can't tell which. In Gowanus, most counted trucks are on 3rd Avenue, a local truck route where they are allowed.

The clearest sign of BQE traffic spilling onto local streets is cars, not trucks: 15,142 vehicles a day go north on Hicks Street between Degraw and Kane, against 1,386 going south. That matches what Streetsblog reported: drivers cutting around the Carroll Gardens trench to rejoin the BQE at Atlantic Avenue. That block falls just outside Carroll Gardens in the Cobble Hill outline, and only 502 of those northbound vehicles were trucks.

Enforcement and complaint data fill in some of the picture. From 2018 to June 2026, NYPD wrote 131 truck route tickets in Carroll Gardens and 4 in Gowanus. From 2020 to 2026, residents filed 15 and 11 truck route complaints with 311. Neither is a count of trucks.

The two bkcb6.app pages lay it all out. The CB6-wide BQE page covers the whole corridor and every CB6 neighborhood. The Carroll Gardens and Gowanus page focuses on those two. Both keep trucks on the highway apart from trucks on neighborhood streets, and both link every number to its source.

What this page means by Carroll Gardens and Gowanus

Neighborhood lines in New York are not official, and people draw them differently. Every count on this page uses the two outlines drawn here, the same ones used on all bkcb6.app maps (city-neighborhoods.geojson), clipped to Community District 6. A number "in Carroll Gardens" means a record whose location falls inside the orange outline; "in Gowanus" means inside the navy one.

  • Carroll Gardens runs corner to corner from Smith St and Hamilton Ave, then Hamilton Ave near Rapelye St, then Hicks St and Degraw St, then Court St and Degraw St, then Court St near Baltic St, then Hoyt St and Baltic St, then Hoyt St and 4th St, then Smith St and 5th St, in straight lines between those points. The BQE runs along its west side.
  • Gowanus runs from Smith St and Hamilton Ave, then Smith St and 5th St, then Hoyt St and 4th St, then Hoyt St and Baltic St, then 4th Ave and Baltic St, then 4th Ave and President St, then 4th Ave and 17th St, then 3rd Ave and Prospect Ave. It includes the Gowanus Canal and the 3rd Avenue truck route.
  • Not included: Hicks Street north of Degraw Street, where the BQE cut-through happens, falls in the Cobble Hill outline. Red Hook, the Columbia Street Waterfront District and Park Slope have their own outlines. Records outside both outlines are counted only in the CB6 and citywide figures.

Corner names are the nearest streets NYC Planning Labs Geosearch returns for each corner point (each links to that lookup).

How the BQE connects to these streets

The BQE is the biggest truck road next to Carroll Gardens and Gowanus. Truck routes are the rules for what those trucks may do when they leave the highway.

  • Trucks passing through should stay on the BQE. The BQE and the Gowanus Expressway are through truck routes. A truck with no stop in Brooklyn "must" use through routes (NYC DOT, Truck Routing), so it has no reason to be on a Carroll Gardens or Gowanus street.
  • Trucks with a stop nearby get off at the ramps. The exits and entrances nearby are listed under How trucks get from the highway to these streets. From a ramp, a truck must use local truck routes such as Hamilton Avenue, 3rd Avenue and 4th Avenue until it is close to its stop. These ramps and routes are labeled on the map.

What a truck route is, and what changes on October 4

New York City tells big trucks which streets they may drive on. These streets are the truck route network, set in the city's traffic rules (34 RCNY §4-13, the city truck route rule) and mapped by NYC DOT (NYC DOT's truck route data). Every other street is off limits to a truck unless it is making a stop there.

The BQE next to Carroll Gardens and Gowanus

"Built between 1937 and 1964, the BQE is Brooklyn's only interstate highway and plays a major role in moving people and goods locally and regionally" (NYC DOT, BQE Central Project).

  • Which part of the BQE this is. Carroll Gardens and Gowanus sit along BQE South, which NYC DOT describes as running "from Atlantic Avenue to the Verrazzano Bridge" and which the state owns (BQE Corridor Vision). Along Carroll Gardens the highway runs below street level in what Streetsblog calls "the Carroll Gardens trench" (Streetsblog, March 20, 2023); to the south it continues as the highway DOT's truck route data names the Gowanus Expressway, along the southwest edge of Gowanus, as the map shows (NYC DOT's truck route data).
  • Trucks on this stretch. DOT's count on the BQE between Kane Street and Sackett Street, at the north edge of Carroll Gardens, found 5,628 trucks a day heading to Queens (2,406 of them heavy) and 6,101 heading to Staten Island (2,210 heavy), on April 4 to 5, 2023.
  • Where the trucks come from. "Of all morning rush-hour truck trips going north on the triple-cantilever, approximately 50 percent originate from the Southwest Brooklyn Industrial Business Zone alone" (BQE Expert Panel Report, January 2020, p. 9 of the PDF). The triple cantilever is in BQE Central, "between Atlantic Avenue to Sands Street" (BQE Corridor Vision), north of Carroll Gardens, so trucks coming up the BQE from the south pass these neighborhoods on the way.
  • Two lanes on the cantilever. DOT says it has limited stress on the structure by "reducing traffic to two lanes on the triple cantilever" (NYC DOT, BQE Central Project); Streetsblog wrote in June 2024 that "former Mayor Bill de Blasio reduced the BQE from three lanes in each direction to two three years ago" (Streetsblog, June 20, 2024). Streetsblog reported "Department of Transportation officials saying that the extra traffic on side streets slowed travel speeds by as much as 30-50 percent in neighborhoods near the Robert Moses-era roadway following the conversion" (Streetsblog, March 20, 2023).
  • Weight sensors are north of here. "The sensors are only deployed on the city-owned section of the BQE between Atlantic Avenue and Sands Street in Brooklyn" (amNY, November 9, 2023). A spokeswoman told FreightWaves, "We are working with the NYPD to enforce this on surrounding streets to prevent trucks from hopping off the BQE and back on again" (FreightWaves, August 1, 2024).
  • What the city says would happen without repairs. DOT: "Further delay would result in removing trucks or all traffic from the highway and diverting them to local streets, creating unsafe conditions and severely impacting quality-of-life in surrounding neighborhoods" (NYC DOT, BQE Central Project). The Mayor's Office says every year of inaction "threatens highway closures that would force dangerous through traffic," including "13,000 daily truck trips," onto local streets (NYC Mayor's Office, August 2026). In a 2024 federal application DOT wrote that closure would mean "displacing 13,000 daily trucks onto local streets" (Streetsblog, March 21, 2024).

How trucks get from the highway to these streets

Trucks leave the highway at exit ramps and are supposed to stay on designated truck routes until they are close to where they are going.

  • Ways off the highway within about 150 meters of Carroll Gardens ("ET" and "EXIT" mean exit): BROOKLYN QUEENS EXPWY ET 26 WB; GOWANUS EXPRESSWAY EXIT 25 EB; GOWANUS EXPRESSWAY EXIT 26 EB (NYC DOT's truck route data).
  • Ways onto the highway within about 150 meters of Carroll Gardens ("EN" and "ENTRANCE" mean entrance): BQE EB ENTRANCE HAMILTON AVE NB; BQE EB ENTRANCE HAMILTON AVE SB; GOWANUS EP WB EN HUGH CAREY TNNL; GOWANUS EXPWY WB EN HAMILTON AVE; HUGH L CAREY TUNNEL NB EN BQE (NYC DOT's truck route data).

Trucks on the wrong routes

A truck is on the wrong route when it drives on a street that is not a designated truck route without a stop to make there, or when a truck with no stop in Brooklyn uses local routes instead of through routes. DOT's rules allow a truck off the network only "for the purpose of arriving at his or her destination," and only by "the most direct route" (NYC DOT, Truck Routing). So a truck on a street that is not a truck route may be breaking the rules, or may be making a legal delivery on that block.

Where the boundaries come from

  • Blocks. The street centerline between two cross streets as the city draws it (NYC Street Centerline); pedestrian paths and highway ramps do not count as cross streets.
  • 100 feet. This page's own choice, not a city definition: it reaches across each intersection and to the building fronts on both sides. Records within 100 feet of two blocks count on the nearer one, so the blocks add up.
  • Districts. From the city's boundary files; a block is assigned to the district its midpoint falls in, and a block a boundary runs through lists both.
