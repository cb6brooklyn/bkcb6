Dean & Bergen Bike Boulevard

Bergen and Dean Streets from Court Street to East New York Avenue: the plan, how it is reviewed, and what the public data shows along the corridor

← Transportation

The story so far

In May the Mayor announced bike boulevards on Bergen and Dean Streets. The city has not yet published a design, a map of where the changes would go, or a schedule beyond "later this year." In the meantime, this page gathers what is known: what was announced, what is on these streets today, what the public record shows, and how the decision will be made.

What was announced. On May 6, 2026, Mayor Zohran Mamdani joined the Bergen Bike Bus's ride and, with NYC DOT Commissioner Mike Flynn, announced that Bergen Street and Dean Street, "from Court Street to East New York Avenue," 10 miles of parallel east-west streets, will be redesigned as bike boulevards, with "a design proposal later this year" and "the first phase targeted for installation in 2027" (NYC Mayor's Office, May 6, 2026). The city's own definition: "low-speed, low-traffic streets that prioritize bicycle and micromobility travel and pedestrian comfort while maintaining local vehicle access," built from "shared, standard, or protected bike lanes, along with treatments that slow vehicle speeds, reduce through traffic, and improve safety for pedestrians and cyclists" (NYC DOT project page; the Street Design Manual's entry is read in full below).

The back story. The announcement was made on a ride of the Bergen Bike Bus, a volunteer group, not a city program, that rides with children to school on Bergen Street on Wednesday mornings; the group describes the route as running from Rockaway Avenue to P.S. 372 at Bond and Court Streets (Bergen Bike Bus, from the group's own site). The Mayor's Office called it "the city's most popular 'bike bus,' a weekly caravan of parents and students traveling to school together," and the Mayor said its families "have for years strapped on their helmets and pulled out their bikes to show the need for better cycling infrastructure" (Mayor's Office, Streetsblog, May 6, 2026). The group has no formal role in the review described below. The streets were chosen, DOT says, because they are "key east-west residential corridors already heavily used by cyclists" (DOT); both feed the Brooklyn and Manhattan bridges, and west of Flatbush Avenue Streetsblog reported "far more bike traffic than car traffic" (Streetsblog). Today Bergen has a bike lane in the DOT file on 36 of its 38 blocks and Dean on 26 of its 36, nearly all of them painted, not protected (calculated from NYC DOT bike routes; by block).

The bus that made room for it. The B65 runs today on Bergen (westbound) and Dean (eastbound). The MTA's draft Brooklyn bus redesign would move it "to travel on Atlantic Av in both directions between Downtown Brooklyn and Washington Av" and discontinue service on "Dean St and Bergen St west of Washington Av" (MTA draft plan, no effective date). Streetsblog reported that the reroute "frees one mile on Dean Street and 1.5 miles on Bergen Street for a bike boulevard design that won't put cyclists in conflict with buses" (Streetsblog, May 11, 2026). The Mayor's Office said the project is being coordinated with the bus redesign, the Flatbush Avenue busway and the Atlantic Avenue Mixed Use Plan (Mayor's Office).

What people have said. News 12 found "praise and neighborhood pushback": a cyclist called the streets into Downtown Brooklyn "absolutely a mess," and a store owner said "I'm all for bike lanes and bike safety," but "the delivery truck can't park next to the curb because the bike lane is next to the curb, so that could be problematic" (News 12 Brooklyn, May 8, 2026). DOT told News 12 the design would include shared, standard or fully protected lanes "while continuing to accommodate vehicle access and parking." On DOT's public feedback map, 1,180 comments had been pinned between May 6, 2026 and September 26, 2026, led by the categories Bike (273), Pedestrian (120) and Unsafe Crossing (113) (DOT feedback map; every comment by category, month and block).

What the record shows so far. Loading the crash record…; the Mayor's Office cites DOT's finding that protected bike lanes cut deaths and serious injuries 18.1 percent overall and 29.2 percent for pedestrians (Mayor's Office). Nothing has been designed yet: "It is even unclear which stretches of the roadways will get the treatment" (Streetsblog), and DOT's project page holds no drawings, limits or dates (DOT).

How it gets decided, and what this page is for. A street redesign of this size is a "major transportation project" under the city's Administrative Code: DOT sends notice to each affected community board, Council member, Assembly member and State Senator, who have 10 business days to comment; a board can ask for a presentation; DOT then decides. There is no vote by a community board, the Borough President, City Planning or the Council (NYC Admin. Code §19-101.2; the steps, against ULURP). The corridor crosses 4 community boards, 6 Council districts, 5 Assembly districts, 3 Senate districts, 3 Congressional districts and 4 police precincts, all shown on the map. Brooklyn Community Board 6 built this page so that anyone, for or against, can see the same facts: every number links to the public data or document it comes from, and the map lets you pick any block, any stretch between two cross streets, or any address and see what is there today, what is proposed, and what people have said about it.

What this page counts

Every number on this page links to the public data or document it comes from. Nothing here is a projection of the final design, which the city has not released.

  • The corridor. The city announced bike boulevards on "the Bergen and Dean Street corridors from Court Street to East New York Avenue in Brooklyn," spanning 10 miles (NYC Mayor's Office, May 2026). This page maps Bergen Street (4.67 miles) and Dean Street (4.44 miles) as drawn in the NYC street centerline file (lengths calculated).
  • "Within 100 feet." Crashes and 311 requests are counted if their mapped point is within 100 feet of the street's centerline, which takes in the intersections with cross streets. Each count links to the NYC Open Data query that returns it, drawn with that same 100-foot outline.
  • Dates. From January 1, 2020 through the latest record: crashes through June 11, 2026, 311 through September 26, 2026. The start date follows the 311 file, which is published as 311 Service Requests from 2020 to Present; the crash file reaches back further but is cut at the same date so the two match.

Where the boundaries come from

Which lines on this page are the city's and which are this page's own choices.

  • Court Street to East New York Avenue: the city's. Those limits are the Mayor's Office's words (NYC Mayor's Office, May 2026). They are also the full length of both streets: in the NYC Street Centerline Bergen Street's first block is Court Street to Boerum Place and its last is Mother Gaston Boulevard to East New York Avenue; Dean Street's first is Court Street to Boerum Place and its last is Sackman Street to East New York Avenue (calculated). So "the corridor" here is every block of both streets, nothing added or left out.
  • 100 feet: this page's choice. The city has not published a boundary for the project's data, so this page draws one: a band 100 feet either side of each street's centerline, from the centerline file. 100 feet reaches across the intersection with any cross street (an avenue roadbed is 30 to 60 feet wide) and to the building fronts on both sides, without reaching the next parallel street (Bergen and Dean are about 250 feet apart, calculated from the centerline). It is a convention, not a city definition; a wider band would count more, a narrower one less, and every count links to the query that uses it so anyone can redraw it.
  • Districts: the city's boundary files. Community districts, Council, Assembly, Senate, Congressional and precinct lines come from the city's published boundary files (district boundary files on bkcb6.app); a block is assigned to the district its midpoint falls in (this page's rule, stated where it is used).
  • Nearest block: this page's rule. A crash at an intersection is within 100 feet of two blocks; the block-by-block counts give it to the nearer one so the blocks add up to the corridor. The corridor totals for Bergen and Dean are counted directly with the 100-foot band and do not depend on that rule.

The plan

In May 2026 the Mayor's Office announced bike boulevards on "the Bergen and Dean Street corridors from Court Street to East New York Avenue in Brooklyn" (NYC Mayor's Office).

  • What a bike boulevard may include: "protected bike lanes, sidewalk extensions, medians, traffic-calming measures and pedestrian improvements" (NYC Mayor's Office).
  • Timeline: the city "plans to release a design proposal later this year, with the first phase targeted for installation in 2027" (NYC Mayor's Office).
  • What is not decided: Streetsblog reported "It is even unclear which stretches of the roadways will get the treatment," and "Details of the design have not been finalized" (Streetsblog, May 6, 2026).
  • Reactions on the street: News 12 quoted a cyclist who said "these streets in particular leading into Downtown Brooklyn are absolutely a mess," and a store owner who said "The delivery truck can't park next to the curb because the bike lane is next to the curb, so that could be problematic" (News 12 Brooklyn, May 8, 2026).

NYC DOT's project page, feedback map, and what DOT means by "bike boulevard"

DOT runs the project from three places: a project page, a feedback map where anyone can pin a comment, and the Street Design Manual entry that defines the treatment. This section reads each one as it stood on September 27, 2026, quotes it, and says what it does not yet contain.

1. The project page

nycdotprojects.info/project/dean-bergen-bike-boulevard-project

  • What it says, in full. "NYC DOT is advancing a community-driven, phased project to transform Dean St and Bergen St into safe, comfortable bike boulevards. As key east-west residential corridors already heavily used by cyclists, these streets are well suited for this approach. Bike boulevards are low-speed, low-traffic streets that prioritize bicycle and micromobility travel and pedestrian comfort while maintaining local vehicle access. Design elements may include shared, standard, or protected bike lanes, along with treatments that slow vehicle speeds, reduce through traffic, and improve safety for pedestrians and cyclists, particularly at intersections. The final design will be shaped through ongoing community engagement across multiple project phases." (DOT). The page's one image is captioned "Bergen Bike Bus."
  • What it does not say. As of September 27, 2026 the page has no project limits, no phase list, no schedule, no design drawings, no presentation, no meeting dates and no document links; its only tool is the feedback map below. The timeline the public has is the Mayor's Office's: a design proposal "later this year" and "the first phase targeted for installation in 2027" (see The plan).
  • Three design options are on the table. "Shared, standard, or protected bike lanes" is DOT's own range: a shared lane (markings only), a standard painted lane, or a protected lane. Which applies where is what the design proposal will decide; DOT has not said.
  • "Maintaining local vehicle access" and "reduce through traffic." Those two phrases are the definition of a bike boulevard and they bear on the truck route question: local access stays, through traffic is what the treatments discourage. Bergen Street from 3rd to 5th Avenue is a designated local truck route (see Truck routes); nothing on DOT's page addresses it.

2. What "bike boulevard" means in DOT's Street Design Manual

nycstreetdesign.info/geometry/bike-boulevard

  • Definition. "A low-traffic, slow street designed to prioritize bicycle and micromobility travel while maintaining local vehicle access," which "incorporates traffic devices and other calming interventions to enhance safety and provide more comfortable conditions for cycling." Bike boulevards "typically span multiple blocks" as "continuous, connected neighborhood routes" (Street Design Manual).
  • Where DOT uses it. "Generally implemented on non-arterial streets that provide a continuous and direct route for cyclists." The manual marks the treatment's "Usage" as "Limited." The one pictured example is Berry Street in Williamsburg (Street Design Manual).
  • The toolkit the manual points to. The entry cross-references the manual's traffic-calming devices: the raised median, the raised speed reducer and the chicane, plus its "Designing for Micromobility" section. Those are the kinds of physical changes "treatments that slow vehicle speeds" on the project page can mean. The entry does not name diverters (barriers that stop through traffic at an intersection); the project page's "reduce through traffic" is the only reference to that idea on either page (Street Design Manual).
  • Benefits the manual claims. Safety "through reduced vehicle speeds and clearly signed networks"; mobility by "linking schools, parks, transit, workplaces, and commercial areas"; and less congestion "by limiting non-essential vehicle trips" (Street Design Manual).
  • Its own cautions. Plan the connections to schools, parks, shops and transit; schedule regular maintenance; and "maintain emergency vehicle access at all times" (Street Design Manual).

3. The feedback map

nycdotprojects.info/project-feedback-map/dean-bergen-streets-feedback-map

  • What it is. DOT's public comment map for this project: "We want to hear from you! Provide comments by choosing a focus area below and following the instructions that pop up. Please be as specific as you can." A commenter picks a category, drops a pin on the map or names a corner or a block, and writes a comment. Comments are public; DOT says they "are reviewed and, while comments will not be edited by NYCDOT personnel, a comment may be blocked if it violates the comment policy" (DOT). No opening or closing date is posted.
  • Categories offered. Accessibility, Bike, Driving, Electric Bike, Freight, Pedestrian, Public Space and Public Transit, each with dozens of specific types (for example "Bike Lane Protection," "Unsafe Crossing," "Illegal Parking," "Speeding," "Red Light Running," "Trucks in Residential Area") (DOT).
  • How many, and when. 1,180 comments were listed when this page read the map on September 27, 2026 (the map's own counter said 1,177 at the top of the page; comments were still arriving). The first is dated May 6, 2026, the day the Mayor's Office announced the project, and the latest September 26, 2026. By month: May 415, June 253, July 156, August 188, September 168.
  • Where they are. 831 of the 1,180 comments are pinned within 150 feet of a Bergen or Dean block (calculated from each comment's map point); the other 349 are on nearby streets, mostly the avenues that cross the corridor. Turn on the "DOT feedback comments" layer on the map to see every pin; each block's card counts its own.
  • What this page stores. Each comment's category, date, location label and map point, read from DOT's public listing (build_bergendean_feedback.py, dotfeedback.json). The text of the comments stays on DOT's site; the map pins link there. The counts are a tally of what people chose to post, not a survey.

How the plan is reviewed

A street redesign like this one follows the notice-and-comment steps in the city's Administrative Code for "major transportation projects," not the land use review known as ULURP (NYC Admin. Code §19-101.2).

Street redesigns: notice, comment, and DOT's decision

  • What counts. A "major transportation project" alters "4 or more consecutive blocks, or 1,000 consecutive feet of street, whichever is less," with "removal of a vehicular lane or full time removal of a parking lane or addition of vehicular travel lanes," or would "add or remove a bicycle lane of any length" (§19-101.2).
  • Notice. "Prior to the implementation of a major transportation project, the department shall forward notice of such project, including a description of such project, to the affected council member, senator, member of assembly, and community board by electronic mail" (§19-101.2(c)). Along this corridor that is 4 community boards, 6 Council members, 5 Assembly members and 3 State Senators (calculated; see the district tables).
  • Comment window. Within 10 business days of the notice (20 business days for a bike lane notice received between June 20 and August 20), elected officials "may submit recommendations or comments," and a community board may comment, "request a presentation of the major transportation project plan by the department, or both"; the presentation is made "within 30 days" of the request (45 days in the summer window) (§19-101.2(d)).
  • The presentation. It "shall include, at a minimum, the project limits, a description, and a justification of such plan, and a map showing the streets affected" (§19-101.2(e)).
  • The decision. DOT "shall consider recommendations or comments" and "may incorporate changes, where appropriate, into the plan," then may proceed with an amended plan or its original plan (§19-101.2). There is no vote by a community board, the Borough President, the City Planning Commission or the City Council in this process.

For comparison: land use review (ULURP)

"The New York City Charter requires certain actions that are reviewed by the City Planning Commission to undergo a Uniform Land Use Review Procedure (ULURP)" (NYC Department of City Planning), such as rezonings, special permits and city map changes (CB6 Land Use guide).

Buses: the B65 today and in the draft plan

Today the B65 "runs between Downtown Brooklyn and Crown Heights" over 4.4 miles; the draft plan shortens it to 4.1 miles and would reroute it "to travel on Atlantic Av in both directions between Downtown Brooklyn and Washington Av," discontinuing service on "Dean St and Bergen St west of Washington Av" (MTA, B65 Local draft plan).

  • On Bergen and Dean today. The MTA's bus speed data lists B65 timepoints on both streets, such as "BERGEN ST/5 AV" and "DEAN ST/5 AV" (MTA segment speed data).
  • Status. The MTA page presents the change as a draft plan and gives no effective date (MTA).
  • Why it matters for the boulevard. Streetsblog reported the reroute frees "one mile on Dean Street and 1.5 miles on Bergen Street for a bike boulevard design that won't put cyclists in conflict with buses" (Streetsblog, May 11, 2026).

Truck routes

Bergen Street is a designated local truck route from 3rd Avenue to 5th Avenue, in BKCB2 and BKCB6 (4 segments; calculated from NYC DOT truck route data, split by district on the bkcb6.app truck routes map). Dean Street has no truck route segments.

  • What a local route is. "The Local Truck Route Network is designated for trucks with an origin and destination within a borough." A truck may use a street that is not a truck route only "for the purpose of arriving at his or her destination" (NYC DOT, Truck Routing).
  • October 4, 2026. None of the 103 truck route changes on DOT's list is on Bergen or Dean (calculated from NYC DOT, map and list of changes).
  • What is not known. The city has not said whether the boulevard design would change Bergen Street's truck route status (none of the sources above addresses it).

Reading the numbers

  • Intersections. The 100-foot band around Bergen or Dean takes in every intersection along it: 82.8% of the 2,125 mapped crashes from January 2020 to June 2026 are within 100 feet of an intersection (calculated from the crash points), and those include crashes between vehicles on the cross street. A district-wide rate spreads intersection crashes across all streets, so the corridor's per-mile figure is not on its own evidence that Bergen or Dean is more dangerous than a typical street; the cyclist and pedestrian counts and the per-block ranking are the better comparison.
  • What a crash record is. Where police placed the crash; "cyclists injured" counts injuries, not who caused the crash. Tap any crash on the map for its record.

Open questions

Questions the public record does not yet answer, with where each comes from.

  • Which stretches, and what design? "It is even unclear which stretches of the roadways will get the treatment" (Streetsblog); the city says a design proposal is coming "later this year" (Mayor's Office).
  • How the notices will be divided. The Admin. Code notice goes to each affected community board and elected official (§19-101.2); the city has not said whether the corridor will be noticed as one project or in phases.
  • Deliveries and curb access. A store owner raised whether delivery trucks could reach the curb if a bike lane runs next to it (News 12). Bergen Street is a local truck route from 3rd to 5th Avenue.
  • Parking. None of the city documents above says how much curb parking a design would keep or remove.
  • When the B65 moves. The MTA's B65 page is a draft plan with no effective date (MTA).
