How ULURP works

The Uniform Land Use Review Procedure is the public review the City Charter requires before a rezoning, a special permit, the sale or purchase of City property and other major land use decisions can take effect. It runs on a fixed clock, and the community board goes first. Tap each step, or drag the slider to see where a project is on any given day.

Who really decides

Member deference

On paper, the City Council's vote on a ULURP application is a vote of all 51 members. In practice, the whole Council votes the way the member whose district the project is in wants it to. That practice is called member deference, and it is not in the City Charter at all.

"Under member deference, the Council gives each councilmember the power to decide the fate of land use proposals in their district. If a local member decides against a proposal, other members of the Council will agree to oppose it, and the proposal will be rejected. If the local member opts to support the proposal, it will be approved."2025 Charter Revision Commission Final Report, p. 39

How the Council vote came to decide it

1975: ULURP is created, with community boards at the start of the process. It ends at the Board of Estimate: the Mayor, Comptroller and City Council President (two votes each) and the five borough presidents (one each). Citywide officials held six of eleven votes.
1989: The U.S. Supreme Court declares the Board of Estimate unconstitutional. The 1989 Charter puts the City Council, 51 members with one vote each, at the end of ULURP, with review over the full range of land use actions. Mayor Koch warned that commission the change would let any member block a project in their district: "We would run the risk of land use paralysis."
1990s: Land use decisions are firmly controlled by Speaker Peter Vallone, who supported multiple rezonings over the wishes of local members.
After 2000: Members are overruled less and less. Practitioners point to term limits, Council rules changes that weakened the Speaker, and rising development pressure.
2009: The last housing project approved through ULURP over the local member's objection.
Today: The Charter still gives the Mayor a veto and the Council an override, but mayoral vetoes are exceedingly rare, so the Council's vote is effectively final, and the local member's position decides it.

A custom, not a rule

Member deference is a matter of custom. The Charter gives each of the 51 members one equal vote on every application that reaches the Council and says nothing about how they decide, so members can trade those votes. Each member gets control over their own district by agreeing not to vote against a colleague's position in theirs. That agreement is where a single member's leverage comes from.

"In essence, member deference is an agreement among councilmembers: each member will control land use in her district, and in return will not second guess the land use decisions of her colleagues."2025 Charter Revision Commission Final Report, p. 39

Demand doesn't stay put

A lot of new homes in one part of the city does not meet the need in the districts that build none. People look for homes near their jobs, schools and families. When a few districts do most of the building, every other district keeps its demand without adding the supply.

"A few neighborhoods produce the majority of affordable housing, others build mainly market-rate housing, and some produce no housing at all. … these dynamics contribute to rising costs, segregation, displacement, and gentrification."2025 Charter Revision Commission Final Report, p. 46

What changed: the 2025 Charter amendments

The 2025 Charter Revision Commission sent five amendments to the ballot on November 4, 2025. Tap each for what it does and how the city voted.

In BKCB6, despite everything people heard during the campaign, the housing measures passed overwhelmingly: 66.2% Yes on Question 2, 65.4% on Question 3 and 62.5% on Question 4. Questions 1 and 6 failed. Voters clearly used discretion.

From the District Manager
Why the ballot measures mattered: Mike RacioppoRead

After millions were poured into campaigns both supporting and opposing the pro-housing ballot measures, voters approved them decisively. These measures removed a councilmember’s ability to veto housing in their district, a power that has been used repeatedly to block housing. The evidence is overwhelming that building housing at the right scale makes a city more affordable, and New Yorkers demonstrated they understand this. Had the ballot measures been defeated, I would have been disappointed, but I would have had to acknowledge that voters do not want to build housing faster. That is not what happened. Voters chose affordability through housing.

And before the assumption creeps in that voters checked “yes” across the board, it is worth noting that there were six ballot questions, and they were not treated equally. Questions 1 and 6 failed. Questions 2, 3, and 4, the housing measures, passed overwhelmingly. Voters were discerning, not disengaged.

The goal is clear: make the city more affordable. Mamdani has committed to building 200,000 affordable housing units. You cannot achieve that with neighborhood vetoes, which is precisely why voters removed them.

Mike Racioppo, District Manager, Brooklyn Community Board 6, and a commissioner on the Charter Revision Commission. From “Why Mamdani’s New ‘Office of Mass Engagement’ Matters,” City Limits, Jan. 16, 2026 ↗

What does a floor area ratio actually build?

Floor area ratio is the total floor area a building may have, divided by the size of its lot. Pick a district and a lot, then change how much of the lot the building covers to see how the same floor area becomes a shorter, wider building or a taller, narrower one.

What this is

Every agency has its own vocabulary and its own rulebook, and most of it is spread across manuals, PDFs and web pages nobody reads end to end. We pulled the glossaries and guides the City publishes into one place, rewrote each entry in plain language, and tagged every one with its policy area, the agencies involved and a link back to where it came from.
