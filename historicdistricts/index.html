NEW YORK CITY LANDMARKS PRESERVATION COMMISSION

Historic districts in Brooklyn Community Board 6

What a historic district is, how an application moves, and every district.

An NYC Historic District is a defined area designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission for its architectural, cultural, or historic significance. Places like large parts of Park Slope and Cobble Hill reflect distinct periods of the city's history, and exterior changes generally require LPC approval to preserve that character. A good rule of thumb is to check the street signs: brown with white lettering usually means you are in a historic district, while green with white lettering usually means you are not. In Brooklyn Community Board 6, that often means we are part of the review process for applications seeking LPC approval, including Certificates of Appropriateness for proposed changes to landmarked properties.

We have three of them: Cobble Hill (1969), Park Slope (1973) and Carroll Gardens (1973). Cobble Hill was extended once, in 1988, and Park Slope twice, in 2012 and 2016.

How to tell you are in a historic district

Check the street signs. Brown with white lettering usually means you are in a historic district. Green with white lettering usually means you are not.

The brown signs came in 1989, on the 25th anniversary of the Landmarks Law, in a terracotta chosen to blend with limestone and brownstone. One side of a street can be in a district and the other side out, so the two sides can carry different colors.

On the map, tap a district for its numbers. Zoom in and the brown signs appear on the streets inside each one.

Certificates of Appropriateness and legalizations

Required for any exterior work on a landmarked building or property in a historic district.

  1. Application. Owner submits drawings, photos, and material samples to LPC.
  2. CB6 Review. Landmarks & Land Use Committee reviews and recommends. Full board votes. Advisory, but part of the official record.
  3. LPC Public Hearing. Presented to all 11 commissioners. Public may testify.
  4. Commission Vote. Approve, modify, or deny.
  5. Permit Issued. If approved, work can proceed.

Not everything needs a hearing. When the work follows the Commission's rules, its staff issue the permit: a Permit for Minor Work or a Certificate of No Effect. That covers 95% of approvals. A Certificate of Appropriateness is for work that does not meet the rules, so the full Commission decides at a public hearing, and that is what comes to us first.

A legalization is an application to approve work already done without a permit. If the work complies with the Commission's rules, the Commission legalizes it and issues the permit after the fact. If it does not, the owner can change the work or ask the full Commission to legalize it, and that request comes through CB6 the same way.

Our vote is advisory. The Commission says it pays careful attention to the community board's opinion but is not required to follow it. Our resolution is part of the official record.

Bringing an application to Community Board 6

Work in a historic district that needs a Certificate of Appropriateness from the Landmarks Preservation Commission (LPC) must be presented to Brooklyn Community Board 6 before the LPC public hearing. CB6’s Landmarks, Land Use & Housing Committee reviews these applications.

1. Get on the agenda

  • Email Mike Racioppo at Mike@bkcb6.org once LPC tells you the application is ready for an LPC public hearing. You will be told which committee meeting you are slated for.
  • You are on the agenda only once CB6 confirms in writing.
  • Say what you are applying for by finishing this sentence: “[Address]: Application for a Certificate of Appropriateness (or a legalization, or whatever else you are applying for) that would allow for …” Include your LPC docket number.

2. Post the public notice

  • CB6 will send you the public notice once it has your description.
  • Post it on the four corners of the property’s block by the deadline you are given.
  • Distribute it to adjacent neighbors. Every neighbor who could see the project is a good rule of thumb.
  • Email photos of the posted notices to Mike@bkcb6.org.

3. Send your presentation

  • Send the LPC-approved presentation (PDF or PPTX) ahead of the meeting, along with LPC staff approval (the LPC Portico Materials Checklist).
  • It must be the material prepared for LPC, presented exactly as is. What goes to the final LPC vote must be exactly what was presented to CB6.

4. Rooftop additions: physical mockup required

  • If the work includes a roof extension, a physical and accurate mockup of the extension’s volume and height is required, including any chimney extensions, marked with orange netting.
  • Include photos of the mockup from the roof and views from the street in your presentation, even if the mockup is not visible from the street.

5. The meeting

  • Committee meetings are on Zoom. CB6 will send Zoom panelist links to everyone presenting; send their names and emails.
  • The committee makes a recommendation, and it goes to the Full Board for a vote. CB6’s resolution is sent to LPC before its hearing.

Questions: Mike@bkcb6.org

The wider argument: preservation and the cost of housing

Designation raises the price of a home. That is the finding of the main study of New York City, by the NYU Furman Center with the National Bureau of Economic Research. It followed sales from 1974 to 2009 and found that designation raises property values inside historic districts outside Manhattan, and raises them for the properties just outside the boundary too. It also found a modest drop in new construction inside districts after designation.

The sale records here point the same way. From 2016 through 2025, a one- to three-family house in a Community Board 6 historic district sold for a median of $3.89 million. Outside the districts the median was $2.48 million. That is 57% more. Inside Park Slope alone, district houses sold for 35% more than the rest of the neighborhood.

The districts are also losing homes. Since 2010, owners combining apartments have removed 384 homes from the six designations, 1.58 for every 1,000 homes each year. That is about twice the rate in the rest of Community Board 6 (0.74). Counting everything built and everything removed since 2010, the six are down 241 homes: 146 added and 387 removed.

Put the two together. Each four-family building that becomes a one-family house is three fewer homes for anyone looking. So the same blocks cost more to buy into and hold fewer places to live. Owners gain from the higher values. People looking for a home pay them, and have fewer homes to choose from. That is the cost side of preservation.

What the StreetEasy listings show. Our file of StreetEasy sale listings has 217 homes on the market in Community Board 6, each at its address: 47 inside a historic district and 170 outside. Compared like for like, the district costs more, though on a small number of listings. A two-bedroom was listed at a median of $1.71 million inside a district (17 listings) and $1.40 million outside (35 listings), 22% more. The overall medians run the other way, $1.85 million inside and $2.44 million outside, because what comes up for sale inside the districts is mostly small apartments: 27 of the 47 district listings have one or two bedrooms, against 57 of 170 outside. Larger homes are a smaller share of what is listed inside the districts.

Homes lost

Most homes lost in the historic districts are lost one building at a time, when an owner combines apartments, such as a four-family building becoming a one-family house. The Department of Buildings records each of these as an alteration that lowers the number of homes.

The loss runs faster inside the historic districts. From January 2010 to July 2026, alterations removed 384 homes in 218 buildings across the six designations, 1.58 for every 1,000 homes each year. In Community Board 6 outside the historic districts they removed 620 homes in 373 buildings, a rate of 0.74.

Sale prices

These are the city's own sale records: every sale the Department of Finance recorded in Community Board 6 from 2016 through 2025. Of 15,713 records, 11,233 are real sales. The rest are transfers for under $10,000, which Finance treats as changes of ownership without a sale, and they are left out. Each sale is placed in or out of a historic district by its location.

Houses sell for more inside the districts. A one- to three-family house in a historic district sold for a median of $3.89 million. Outside the districts the median was $2.48 million. That is 57% more. Condos sold for 12% more inside the districts. Co-ops sold for about the same.

StreetEasy listings. StreetEasy reports prices by neighborhood, not by building, so it cannot split a neighborhood into district and non-district. For single-family homes its medians are highest in Cobble Hill and Park Slope, the two neighborhoods with the most historic district property. For all home types together the pattern does not hold: Carroll Gardens, with 6% of its properties in a district, has the highest median sale price. The mix of houses, condos and co-ops differs from one neighborhood to the next.
