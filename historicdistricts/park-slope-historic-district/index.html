What a historic district is

A historic district is an area the Landmarks Preservation Commission (LPC) has designated because it has architectural and historical significance and a distinct "sense of place." To qualify, the buildings must represent at least one period or style of architecture typical of an era in the city's history, have a distinct sense of place and form a coherent streetscape.

It is one of four kinds of designation. The others are individual landmarks (the outside of one structure), interior landmarks (interiors customarily open to the public) and scenic landmarks (city-owned parks and landscapes, such as Prospect Park).

The city has more than 38,000 landmark properties. Most are in its 157 historic districts and district extensions.

The street signs tell you. Inside a historic district the street signs are terracotta brown with white lettering, where the usual sign is green. The brown signs came in 1989, on the 25th anniversary of the Landmarks Law, and the color was chosen to blend with the city's limestone and brownstone buildings. One side of a street can be in a district and the other side out, so the two sides can carry different colors.

How historic districts came to be

The Landmarks Preservation Commission was created in 1965 by legislation signed by Mayor Robert F. Wagner. It was a response to the loss of historically significant buildings, most notably Pennsylvania Station. The station, designed by McKim, Mead & White and completed in 1911, covered two full blocks from 31st to 33rd Streets between Seventh and Eighth Avenues. It was demolished in 1963 to make way for Madison Square Garden.

The Landmarks Law says the purpose of protecting these buildings and places is to:

  • Stabilize and improve property values
  • Foster civic pride
  • Protect and enhance the city's attractions to tourists
  • Strengthen the city's economy
  • Promote the use of historic districts and landmarks for the education, pleasure and welfare of the people of the city

The Commission is required by the City Charter. It has 11 commissioners appointed by the Mayor and a staff of about 80.

How a district gets designated:

  1. Survey. LPC's Research Department identifies candidates, including places the public suggests.
  2. Calendaring. The full Commission votes at a public meeting on whether to schedule a public hearing.
  3. Report. Research staff write a detailed report. Owners get a draft to review.
  4. Public hearing. Anyone may testify. Notice goes to owners, City Planning, the community board and elected officials.
  5. Commission vote. Six of the 11 commissioners must vote yes. Designation takes effect on that vote.
  6. City Planning Commission. It has 60 days to report to the City Council on what the designation means for zoning and city plans. For a historic district it must hold its own public hearing first.
  7. City Council. It has 120 days to modify or disapprove the designation by majority vote. Its approval is not required. The Mayor can veto the Council's vote within five days, and the Council can override by a two-thirds vote within 10 days.

In Brooklyn Community Board 6: Cobble Hill came first (December 30, 1969), then Park Slope (July 17, 1973) and Carroll Gardens (September 25, 1973). The Cobble Hill Extension followed (June 7, 1988), then the Park Slope Extension (April 17, 2012) and Park Slope Extension II (April 12, 2016).

Historic districts and zoning

A historic district is not a zoning district. The two come from different laws and different agencies.

  • Zoning is the system of district-based rules that decides what can be built on a lot, how large and for what purpose. It lives in the Zoning Resolution, in place since 1961.
  • Landmark rules live in the Landmarks Law, which is Title 25, Chapter 3 of the city's Administrative Code. They do not set what can be built. They require LPC's permission to change what is there.

Both apply at once. A project that zoning allows still needs an LPC permit if it touches the exterior of a building in the district.

Where the two meet:

  • When a district is designated, the City Planning Commission reports to the City Council on the designation's effect on zoning.
  • A limited height district can be laid over a historic district to cap building height. The city has mapped them on the Upper East Side, in Gramercy Park, Brooklyn Heights and Cobble Hill.
  • Contextual zoning (districts ending in A, B, D or X) regulates height, bulk, setback and street frontage so new buildings match the existing neighborhood character.
  • A transfer of development rights can move unused building rights from a landmark site to another lot. It needs approval from LPC and the City Planning Commission.

What the Landmarks Preservation Commission does, and how

After designation, LPC regulates changes to the buildings. In its own words it does not prevent owners from making changes. It works with them so the changes are appropriate to the character and style of the building and the district.

An LPC permit is required for:

  • Any restoration, replacement, alteration, reconstruction, demolition or new construction that affects the exterior of a building in a historic district
  • Any work on the exterior, even on parts that cannot be seen from the street
  • Anything built within the district's boundaries, such as a new building on a vacant lot
  • Interior work that needs a Department of Buildings permit, or that affects the exterior, such as vents and louvers

The LPC permit is required even when the Department of Buildings needs no permit for the job.

No permit is needed for emergency and routine upkeep such as replacing broken glass in kind, replacing window and door hardware, re-caulking, touching up paint in the same color, and boarding up or tarping damage until permanent repairs are approved.

Who decides:

  • LPC staff issue the permit when the work follows the Commission's rules. That covers 95% of approvals. These are the Permit for Minor Work and the Certificate of No Effect, with faster versions for some work (FasTrack and the Expedited Certificate of No Effect).
  • The full Commission decides at a public hearing when the work does not meet the rules. The owner makes the case that it is appropriate. The approval is a Certificate of Appropriateness.
  • After approval, LPC can amend a permit and issues a Notice of Compliance when the work is signed off.

Applications are filed online through LPC's Portico system. The permit must be posted at the building while the work is under way.

Enforcement. Work without a permit, or not in line with one, is a violation, even if a prior owner did it. LPC usually starts with a warning letter, then a summons with a hearing date. It can issue a stop work order when illegal work is under way.

Brooklyn Community Board 6's part

Work that needs a Certificate of Appropriateness must be presented to Brooklyn Community Board 6 before the LPC public hearing. The Landmarks, Land Use & Housing Committee reviews it and makes a recommendation, the Full Board votes, and the resolution goes to LPC before its hearing. The vote is advisory. LPC is not bound by it, but it is part of the official record.

  1. Get on the agenda. Email Mike@bkcb6.org once LPC says the application is ready for a public hearing. Finish this sentence: "[Address]: Application for a Certificate of Appropriateness (or a legalization, or whatever else you are applying for) that would allow for ..." Include the LPC docket number. You are on the agenda only once CB6 confirms in writing.
  2. Post the public notice. CB6 sends the notice. Post it on the four corners of the property's block by the deadline, give it to adjacent neighbors (every neighbor who could see the project is a good rule of thumb), and email photos of the posted notices.
  3. Send the presentation. Send the LPC-approved presentation ahead of the meeting with LPC staff approval (the LPC Portico Materials Checklist). It must be exactly what goes to the LPC vote.
  4. Rooftop additions need a physical mockup of the extension's volume and height, including chimney extensions, marked with orange netting, with photos from the roof and from the street.
  5. The meeting. Committee meetings are on Zoom. The committee recommends, the Full Board votes, and CB6's resolution is sent to LPC before its hearing.
