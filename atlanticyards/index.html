    What it is: an arena, a railyard and 16 towers

    A state project, not a city one. Empire State Development, the state’s economic development agency, approved it in 2006, and its plan overrides city zoning, so it never went through the city’s land use process.

    As approved, Atlantic Yards is a 22-acre, $4.9 billion project: the 18,000-seat Barclays Center, a rebuilt Long Island Rail Road yard and subway upgrades, 16 residential and commercial buildings with up to 6,430 apartments, 2,250 of them affordable, and eight acres of publicly accessible open space. The site is bounded by Atlantic Avenue to the north, Dean Street to the south, Vanderbilt Avenue to the east, and Flatbush Avenue and 4th Avenue to the west, in Prospect Heights.

    Phase I is the part on solid ground between Flatbush Avenue and Vanderbilt Avenue south of the railyard: the arena, eight apartment buildings, three acres of open space run by the Pacific Park Conservancy, an updated subway entrance, an upgraded LIRR yard, a public middle school and a health care facility. Phase II is everything left: the two railyard blocks between 6th Avenue and Vanderbilt Avenue, which need a deck built over working tracks before anything can go on them, Site 5 across Flatbush Avenue at 4th Avenue, and B1, the arena plaza.

    The footprint straddles Community Boards 2, 6 and 8. Two of the eight buildings, 461 Dean Street and 38 Sixth Avenue, sit in BKCB6. The arena, 18 Sixth Avenue, the railyard and Site 5 are in BKCB2. Pacific Park, 662 Pacific Street, 550 Vanderbilt Avenue, 535 Carlton Avenue and 595 and 615 Dean Street are in BKCB8.

    The 876: the affordable housing still owed

    The 2,250 affordable apartments were promised in 2005, and a 2014 settlement with the state set May 2025 as the deadline to finish them, with damages of $2,000 a month for every one not built.

    By the deadline, 1,374 were built, leaving 876. The 2014 agreement put the developer on the hook for $2,000 a month per unbuilt unit, up to $21 million a year. In June 2025 the state deferred collecting the damages from Greenland USA, the developer at the time, and in October 2025, when it approved the new development team, it agreed to $12 million for the city’s affordable housing fund instead, $4.5 million of it up front.

    Who the built affordable apartments are for has also been a fight. Most of the 1,374 are for middle-income households: of the 1,303 counted in the city’s housing data, 971 are for middle-income households, including every income-restricted apartment at 18 Sixth Avenue, 662 Pacific Street and 595 Dean Street. Phase II lowers the ceiling for income-restricted apartments from 165% to 120% of area median income.

    Phase II: the plan for the rest

    A new development team, Cirrus Real Estate Partners and LCOR, took over the unbuilt sites in October 2025 and signed a memorandum of understanding with the state on July 28, 2026. It is not binding; the state has to finish an environmental review and amend the project plan first.
    Phase II as proposed, July 28, 2026
    Up to 5,600 new apartments in seven towers, 1,242 of them income-restricted, 27% of the new rentals, at 40% to 120% of area median income, and at least 30% with two or more bedrooms. That brings the project to 8,812 apartments, 2,382 more than approved, and 2,616 income-restricted, 366 more than the 2,250 promised. Heights go up on every site: 799 and 570 feet at Site 5, 684 feet at B7, against 250 feet at Site 5 and 460 feet at B7 in the approved plan. B8 becomes open space instead of a tower.

    What is in it

    • Build on solid ground first. Site 5 and B6, which sits partly on land below Atlantic Avenue, go first because neither waits on a deck over the tracks.
    • Affordability by building. 25% of rentals at an average of 60% of area median income at Site 5 and B6; 27.5% at an average of 80% at B5 and B10; 30% at an average of 90% at B7 and B9, with no income-restricted rent above 120%. All income-restricted apartments are permanent. 400 condominiums at Site 5, 300 at B7 and 300 at B9 have none.
    • Open space. More than five acres in Phase II, for more than eight acres across the project, possibly run by the Pacific Park Conservancy or NYC Parks.
    • Community space. At least 20,000 square feet, including an intergenerational community center in B6 and more space in B5 and B10, plus up to about 140,000 square feet of retail and an optional hotel at Site 5.
    • Public money and taxes. The first railyard deck depends on $350 million from the state, $175 million of it appropriated so far. The rental buildings can get a full property tax exemption for 40 years through payments in lieu of taxes, and Phase II is exempt from sales taxes and from mortgage recording taxes on construction loans.
    • Deadlines. The first deck, Block 1120, done by the end of 2032; Site 5 and the Block 1120 buildings by the end of 2037; Block 1121 by the end of 2040 with a funding agreement and 2042 without one. Each extension costs $500,000 the first year and $1 million the second, paid toward affordable housing.

    Where the review stands

    The state released its Draft Scope of Work for a Draft Second Supplemental Environmental Impact Statement on August 24, 2026, and held a public scoping meeting on Zoom on September 24. Written comments on the scope closed at 5 PM on October 26, 2026. Next, in 2027: the Draft Environmental Impact Statement and the Amended Modified General Project Plan, a public hearing and comment period, then a vote of the Empire State Development board. The state also plans an in-person workshop on the proposed open spaces in November. Sign up for updates by writing to AtlanticYards@esd.ny.gov.

    Who decides

    Empire State Development makes the decisions. The Atlantic Yards Community Development Corporation, created in 2014, reviews changes to the plan and construction impacts and advises the state’s board.
    The AY CDC has 14 directors: nine appointed by the Governor and one each by the State Senate’s President Pro Tem, the Assembly Speaker, the Mayor, the Council Speaker and the Brooklyn Borough President. Phone (212) 803-3736.
