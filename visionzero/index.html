The longer version

Street safety and housing are connected. Building more homes increases density, and density requires better and more environmentally sound ways to move people. That is why pro-housing and safe streets coalitions usually align, and why a bus stop, a bike lane and a rezoning belong in the same conversation.

CB6 has a particular stake in this. Sammy's Law, which gave New York City the authority to lower speed limits below the state minimum, was named for a child killed by a speeding driver in this district. The Court Street protected bike lane, which CB6 voted unanimously to support, drew a lawsuit that a Brooklyn Supreme Court judge dismissed, holding that decisions about how city streets are used are policy questions for elected and accountable officials rather than the courts.

Every line and point here was clipped to Department of City Planning community district boundaries before it was counted, because agency boundary fields are frequently wrong. A segment counts in the district it actually sits in.

What the shorthand means
In the plan
Vision Zero
The city’s commitment, since 2014, to eliminate traffic deaths and severe injuries. Vision Zero Reimagined is the 2026 action plan renewing it, with eight goals and 111 actions across 19 agencies.
Priority Corridor
A street DOT has singled out for safety work because of its crash history. There are 199 citywide. The plan commits to finishing safety treatments on all of them.
Priority Intersection
One of 304 intersections DOT tracks the same way.
Daylighting
Keeping the corners of an intersection clear of parked cars so drivers and people crossing can see each other. Hardened daylighting adds something physical, such as a planter, bollard or bike corral, so it cannot be parked on.
LPI
Leading Pedestrian Interval. The walk signal comes on a few seconds before the light turns green for drivers.
Sammy’s Law
The 2024 State law that let the city set speed limits below 25 mph. The city is putting 15 mph School Slow Zones at every eligible school and the plan proposes 20 mph zones across whole neighborhoods.
ISA
Intelligent Speed Assistance, a device that reads the speed limit and keeps a vehicle from exceeding it. The Super Speeder program will require it for drivers with 16 or more speed camera violations in a year.
Ghost plates
Fake, covered or altered license plates that defeat cameras and tolls.
Major Violator
The plan’s term for a driver in a hit and run, or without a valid license, or with three or more prior convictions, a prior suspension or a DWI. Involved in 52 percent of traffic deaths in 2025.
KSI
Killed or severely injured, the measure DOT uses for its equity tracking.
On this page
Killed, injured
From the NYPD crash reports on NYC Open Data, crashes with at least one person hurt, placed in a district by the crash coordinates. DOT’s official Vision Zero counts, which the plan uses, are reconciled after the fact and run a little lower than the NYPD file.
Speed humps, turn calming, LPIs, SIPs
DOT’s Vision Zero View inventory of what has been installed where. SIP is a Street Improvement Project.
District codes
BKCB6
Brooklyn Community Board 6. The first two letters are the borough, BK Brooklyn, MN Manhattan, QN Queens, BX the Bronx, SI Staten Island. CB and the number are the community board.
Council 39
City Council district 39. There are 51 of them, and they do not line up with community districts.
The ranking behind the map
Why this page exists

On September 15, 2026, Mayor Mamdani’s Office of Operations published Vision Zero Reimagined, a ten-year action plan with eight goals and 111 actions.

Read the rest

Like Block by Block on the housing side, this page puts the city’s plan next to the public record, so anyone can see what the plan commits to, what has happened on the streets of each community district and council district since Vision Zero began in 2014, and what Brooklyn Community Board 6 has already asked for. The board’s positions come straight from its minutes, 2010 to 2026, with a link to each one. Its FY27 Statement of District Needs asked for a district-wide transportation assessment, redesigned priority corridors, hardened universal daylighting, loading zones, more cameras and better enforcement. Several of those are now in the plan.

Nothing here is the board’s opinion of the plan. It is the plan, the crashes and the record, laid side by side.

What the plan says is killing people

What the plan says is killing people

The plan is direct about who is behind the wheel in fatal crashes.
Quoted from the Mayor’s Office report
“Fatalities involving Major Violators grew by 72% between 2018–2019 and 2020–2021, and account for nearly the entire rise in fatalities during the pandemic era.” In 2025, 52 percent of traffic deaths involved a driver in a hit and run, without a valid license, or with prior convictions, a prior suspension or a DWI.

Quoted from the Mayor’s Office report
“Crashes involving automobiles account for 89% of all traffic fatalities, most frequently affecting vulnerable road users outside the motor vehicle.” Heavy vehicles, trucks and buses, were involved in 18 percent of deaths and 25 percent of failure-to-yield pedestrian deaths from 2014 to 2025.
