Sammy’s Law, daylighting and low traffic neighborhoods

Three ways to make streets safer: what each one is, what CB6 has voted for, and where the city stands

← Transportation

On October 14, 2026, Comptroller Mark Levine, Borough Presidents Brad Hoylman-Sigal, Antonio Reynoso and Donovan Richards, and Council Majority Leader Shaun Abreu asked DOT to go district-wide in every district that has asked for 20 mph, citing CB6 among them, with daylighting and low traffic neighborhoods alongside.

CB6 has voted for all three: daylighting in October 2023, a Gowanus low traffic neighborhood pilot in November 2025, and a district-wide 20 mph limit under Sammy’s Law in March 2026. In January 2024, Council Member Shahana Hanif, Borough President Antonio Reynoso, State Senators Andrew Gounardes and Zellnor Myrie, and Assembly Members Marcela Mitaynes, Jo Anne Simon and Robert Carroll wrote to DOT citing our daylighting vote.

We are not alone. 17 community boards have passed district-wide 20 mph resolutions: Manhattan 2 to 9, Bronx 6, Brooklyn 1, 2, 4, 6 and 7, and Queens 1, 2 and 6. 23 have passed daylighting resolutions: Manhattan 1 to 9 and 11, Brooklyn 1, 2, 4, 6, 7 and 8, Queens 1, 2, 6 and 13, and Bronx 5, 6 and 11.

    Sammy’s Law: the speed limit

    The 2024 State law that let New York City set speed limits below 25 mph for the first time. It carries the name of Sammy Cohen Eckstein, 12, killed by a driver on Prospect Park West in 2013.

    What it allows

    Under Sammy’s Law, DOT can lower a street’s limit to 20 mph with posted signs, and to 10 mph where it adds traffic calming, except on roads with three or more travel lanes in each direction outside Manhattan. Before any change the city must give the community board 60 days’ notice and a chance to comment. The Comptroller’s office calls the rest low-cost and quick: new signs, retimed signals and new rules for the speed cameras.

    What CB6 asked for

    Resolution, March 11, 2026 committee unanimous, Feb 25 full board unanimous
    A district-wide 20 mph slow zone on all applicable streets in CB6, with adjusted signal timing and camera enforcement, and a 20 mph limit citywide on all applicable streets. The board’s reasoning: in 2025 CB6 recorded 1,170 crashes, 523 injuries and 3 deaths, and a 20 mph limit meaningfully reduces the likelihood of a fatal outcome. CB6 was one of the first boards in the city to ask; Brooklyn CB1 was the first, in February 2025.

    What the Comptroller and the Borough Presidents asked for

    On October 14, 2026, Comptroller Mark Levine, Borough Presidents Brad Hoylman-Sigal, Antonio Reynoso and Donovan Richards, and Council Majority Leader Shaun Abreu wrote to DOT Commissioner Mike Flynn to expand Sammy’s Law on eligible streets throughout the 17 community districts that have approved district-wide implementation, and to prioritize the districts with the most crashes next. Their case, in order:

    • Lower limits save lives. When the citywide limit went from 30 to 25 mph, total crashes fell 39% on streets with posted signs, and the city halved its fatality rate when crashes occur.
    • The demand is already there. 17 boards have passed district-wide resolutions: Manhattan 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9, Bronx 6, Brooklyn 1, 2, 4, 6 and 7, Queens 1, 2 and 6. Four Borough Presidents and 63 city and state legislators had already signed a letter asking for the same.
    • The need is measurable. Those 17 districts sustained 22,490 collisions, 60 deaths and 11,644 serious injuries in 2025. Since their resolutions passed, 14 people have been killed in them. In CB6, 315 crashes and no deaths in the 4.7 months after the March vote, as of June 15, 2026.
    • School zones are a start, not the plan. The 15 mph zones the Mayor committed to are “an important step in the right direction, but not enough on its own.” Four children were struck and killed by cars in the city this year.
    • It is cheap and fast. An email to the board, 60 days, then signs, signal timing and camera rules.

    The letter asks DOT for a written strategy for using its Sammy’s Law authority. It also names daylighting and low traffic neighborhoods as the complements to lower speed limits, and notes DOT has the authority to do all three.

    Where the city stands

    Since the law took effect in 2024, DOT lowered limits at just over 100 locations, including one regional slow zone per borough. On March 16, 2026 Mayor Mamdani and Commissioner Flynn committed to 15 mph School Slow Zones at every eligible school location in the city, 2,300 locations housing 3,200 schools, by the end of the Mayor’s first term, each one after the 60-day board notice. By September 9, 2026 more than 200 had been installed this year, with roughly 800 expected by the end of 2026. A pedestrian struck at 25 mph is more than three times as likely to be seriously injured as one struck at 15 mph. District-wide 20 mph, the thing CB6 and the other 16 boards asked for, has not been announced for any district.

    Daylighting: the corner

    Keeping the parking spots closest to a corner clear, so people crossing and drivers turning can see each other. New York State law bars parking within 20 feet of a crosswalk; New York City exempted itself.

    What CB6 asked for

    Resolution, October 11, 2023 33 for, 3 against, 2 abstaining
    That the city implement intersection daylighting and opt back into the State law prohibiting parking within 20 feet of an intersection, at all possible intersections, with physical barriers rather than signs and enforcement alone, and support for the Council’s daylighting bill, Intro 854-A, co-sponsored by all three CB6 council members.

    Three months later, on January 16, 2024, Council Member Shahana Hanif, Borough President Antonio Reynoso, State Senators Andrew Gounardes and Zellnor Myrie and Assembly Members Marcela Mitaynes, Jo Anne Simon and Robert Carroll wrote to DOT citing the CB6 vote and asking for universal daylighting with hardened materials: boulders, planters, bike corrals. Their letter notes that Hoboken, with daylighting at every intersection, saw a 30% drop in pedestrian injuries from vehicles, that Local Law 66 of 2023 directs DOT to daylight 100 intersections a year by 2025, and that the Mayor had announced 1,000 intersections for 2024.

    Where it stands

    The Comptroller’s letter counts 23 community boards that have passed daylighting resolutions: Manhattan 1 to 9 and 11, Brooklyn 1, 2, 4, 6, 7 and 8, Queens 1, 2, 6 and 13, and Bronx 5, 6 and 11. Those 23 districts sustained 30,502 collisions, 78 deaths and 16,477 serious injuries in 2025. The Mayor’s 2026 Vision Zero Reimagined plan promises hardened daylighting “or better” at a minimum of 1,000 intersections each year; the Council’s universal daylighting bill, which would require hardened daylighting at 1,000 intersections a year by law, has majority support but has not passed, and DOT opposes it. CB6’s own positions since 2023 are on the Vision Zero Reimagined in CB6 page.

    On the map, Intersections draws CB6’s 504 intersections with at least one injury crash since 2014, sized by injuries, from the NYPD crash data, and Where people were killed marks the 27 fatal crashes in that time. These are the corners daylighting is for.

    Low traffic neighborhood: the block

    A low traffic neighborhood, or LTN, redirects through-traffic away from residential streets while keeping every address reachable for drivers, cyclists and pedestrians. CB6 has asked for one in Gowanus.

    What CB6 asked for

    Resolution, November 12, 2025 15 for, 3 abstaining
    Support for DOT working with the community to develop a pilot LTN bounded roughly by Baltic Street to the north, 2nd Place to the south, Smith Street to the west and 3rd Avenue to the east. The board’s reasoning: the Gowanus rezoning adds more than 8,000 apartments, about 80% of the driving inside the boundary is already cut-through traffic, and only 8% of residents commute by car. CB6 also asked that the Carroll Street Bridge, closed to vehicles, stay closed to them for good and open to people walking and biking as a crossing of the canal.

    Where it stands

    The Carroll Street Bridge is now fully pedestrianized. DOT has not announced a low traffic neighborhood pilot in Gowanus. The Comptroller’s letter lists LTNs with daylighting as the “critical complements to speed limit reductions,” says DOT already has the authority to implement all three, and notes that legislation introduced in the Council would support DOT in developing LTN pilot programs. On the map, the Gowanus pilot area is the boundary in the resolution, drawn from the four streets it names, and the Carroll Street Bridge is marked.
