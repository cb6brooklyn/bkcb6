Zoning and Land Use Explained

Every zoning district, and what can actually be built there

Zoning decides what can be built where. Two things together control it: the zoning district a lot sits in, like R6B or M1-1, and the Use Group a proposed use belongs to. If the Use Group is allowed in that district, it can be built. If not, it needs a special permit, or it cannot be built at all.

In 2024 the City of Yes amendment replaced the old Use Groups 1 through 18 with ten Use Groups numbered I through X. This page shows all ten and where each one is allowed.

This one address does most of the teaching, because four separate layers apply to it at once and none of them substitutes for the others.

1The base district permits the library outright. A public library is Use Group III, a community facility. R6 allows Use Group III as of right. No approval, no hearing, no variance. That is why libraries, schools and houses of worship turn up in the middle of residential blocks: the zoning invites them.
2The overlay is what permits the office. A government or business office is Use Group VII. R6 alone does not allow it, and would require a special permit. The C1-3 overlay mapped along the corridor does allow it. Same lot, same building, different rule doing the work. Strip the overlay and the community board office would be a zoning problem while the library beside it would not.
3Housing could be added tomorrow without a rezoning. R6 permits Use Group II, residences, as of right, in every form including apartment buildings. Nothing about the current civic use forecloses that. A rezoning is a ULURP action with a community board vote; adding housing here would need none of it. This is the point most often missed in conversations about what public sites can become.
4The historic district controls a different question entirely. Cobble Hill Historic District designation does not change what use is allowed. It governs what the building looks like. Exterior work needs a Certificate of Appropriateness from the Landmarks Preservation Commission. Zoning answers what, landmarks answers how it looks, and public ownership answers who decides. Confusing the three is the single most common mistake in a land use conversation.

The transferable lesson: before asking whether something can go somewhere, work out which layer is actually binding. Base district, overlay, special purpose district, landmark status and ownership are five different systems, and an answer under one tells you nothing about the others.
