Robert's Rules, the Short Version

A guide for community board members

← bkcb6.app

Robert's Rules of Order has a reputation for being complicated. It isn't, at least not the part you actually need. Ninety percent of what happens at a board meeting runs on a handful of moves. Learn those and you can follow any meeting, and more importantly, you can participate in one.

By Mike Racioppo

Here is the thing to keep in mind before any of the procedure: the rules exist for one reason, to make sure a group of people can make decisions fairly. They protect the majority's right to act and the minority's right to be heard, and they keep one loud person from running the room. That is the whole philosophy. When you are not sure what to do, ask yourself what is fair and orderly, and you will usually land close to the right answer.

The one cycle that runs everything

Almost every decision a board makes follows the same five beats. Once you see the pattern, meetings stop feeling like a foreign language.

1
Someone makes a motion. "I move that we approve the application." A motion is just a proposal to do something. Nothing happens until one is on the floor.
2
Someone seconds it. "Second." This just means a second person thinks it is worth discussing. It is not agreement. If nobody seconds, the motion dies and we move on.
3
The chair opens debate. Members discuss the motion, one at a time, through the chair. This is where the real work happens.
4
The chair calls the vote. "All in favor. Opposed. Abstentions." Usually a voice vote or a show of hands.
5
The chair announces the result. "The motion carries" or "the motion fails." It is not official until the chair says so.

That is it. Approve a resolution, adopt a budget priority, endorse a liquor license, send a letter. Same five beats every time.

The words to know

Quorum

The minimum number of members who have to be present to do business. No quorum, no votes, full stop. You can still talk, but nothing you decide counts. This is why showing up matters more than people think. A board that cannot make quorum cannot do its job.

Motion

A proposal to take an action. "Main motions" start a new piece of business. There are also motions about other motions, which sounds confusing but mostly comes down to the few below.

Second

A signal that at least one other person wants to discuss the motion. No second, no discussion.

Amend

Changing a motion before the final vote. "I move to amend the motion to add the condition that the promenade be opened to the public." You vote on the amendment first, then on the motion as amended. This is how a board shapes a proposal instead of just taking it or leaving it. It is one of the most useful tools you have, and the most underused.

Table

Setting a motion aside, usually because you need more information or more time. "I move to table this until next month." Tabling is not killing something. It is hitting pause.

Call the question

A request to end debate and vote now. Worth knowing because people say it, but it does not automatically stop discussion, and it should not be used to cut off members who still want to speak. When in doubt, let people talk.

Point of order

"Point of order." You are telling the chair you think a rule is being broken. The chair rules on it. Use it when something is genuinely off the rails, not to score points.

Abstaining

You can vote yes, vote no, or abstain. Abstaining means you are present but choosing not to take a side, maybe because of a conflict or because you genuinely have not made up your mind. It is a legitimate choice. Just know that on most votes an abstention is not a no, it simply is not counted as a yes.

How to actually participate

New members often sit quietly for a few meetings because they are not sure how to jump in. Here is how.

Wait to be recognized by the chair, then speak. You do not interrupt and you do not talk over people. If you want to propose something, say I move that... If you want to change something already on the floor, say I move to amend... If you agree something should be discussed, say Second. If you think a rule is being broken, say Point of order.

That small vocabulary covers the vast majority of what you will ever need to do. You do not have to memorize the thick book. You have to know when it is your turn and what you want to say.

How it really works on a community board

A community board is not a courtroom and it is not the floor of Congress. We use Robert's Rules as a framework, not a weapon. The goal is a fair, orderly meeting where everyone gets heard and the board can actually decide things, not a contest over who knows the most obscure parliamentary trick.

A few things that are specific to how a board like ours operates:

Woodrow Wilson once observed that the legislature in session is the legislature on public exhibition, while the legislature in its committee rooms is the legislature at work. The same is true here. The full board meeting is the part the public sees, but the real work, the close reading of an application, the hard questions, the shaping of a recommendation, happens in committee. If you want to actually influence an outcome, the committee room is where to be.

▸
Committees do the heavy lifting. Most items are debated in detail at the committee level, where a recommendation is shaped. By the time something reaches the full board, there is usually a committee motion already on the floor, and the full board debates and votes on that.
▸
A committee recommendation does not need a second at the full board. It already had support in committee. It comes to the floor as a motion ready for debate.
▸
The public gets to speak, but the public does not vote. Members vote. Public comment is part of the record and it matters, but keep the two roles clear.
▸
Most of our votes are advisory. On land use and liquor licenses, we recommend. The weight of that recommendation comes from a clear vote and a well-reasoned record, which is exactly why the procedure matters.
The bottom line

Do not let the procedure intimidate you, but do respect it. The rules are a tool for fairness, not a test you can fail, and they are also not an invitation to grandstand. The point is not to make a motion for the sake of making one. It is to do your homework, mostly in committee, raise the right question at the right time, and cast an informed vote. Used well, the process makes the board stronger. Used as a stage, it just wastes everyone's evening.
