Twelve years ago New York decided that people dying in traffic is a choice, not an accident. Vision Zero was launched in 2014 under Mayor Bill de Blasio, a Park Slope resident of this district. Since then the city has cut the speed limit to 25 mph, put cameras in school zones and rebuilt hundreds of streets, and traffic deaths have fallen to record lows. 210 people were still killed last year.

On September 15, 2026 the Mayor’s Office published Vision Zero Reimagined, a ten-year plan with eight goals and 111 actions. Read next to what this board has voted for since 2010, the overlap is hard to miss. One thing is still missing, and it is the thing CB6 has asked for four times: universal daylighting.

Mike Racioppo
District Manager, Brooklyn Community Board 6

CB6 has been in this fight the whole time, and often early. The board backed a neighborhood slow zone before Vision Zero existed, voted 33 to nothing for the 25 mph bill months before it became law, asked the city to obey the state daylighting rule two years before the Council took it up, and passed a district-wide 20 mph slow zone six months before the Mayor’s plan proposed neighborhood 20 mph zones. Sammy’s Law carries the name of a child killed on Prospect Park West.

The plan now commits to hardened daylighting, 20 mph zones, curb management and loading zones, protected bike lanes on priority corridors and accountability for delivery platforms. Those are CB6 positions, already voted, now city policy. What it does not do is end the city exempting itself from the state rule that keeps parked cars 20 feet back from a crosswalk. It promises 1,000 intersections a year. There are about 40,000.

Still unfinished
Universal daylighting is not in this plan
New York State bans parking within 20 feet of a crosswalk. New York City exempts itself from that law and has since the exemption was written. CB6 voted to end the exemption in October 2023, then in May 2025 voted for hardened daylighting at every intersection in the city and for the Council bill that would require it. That bill, Intro 1138, reached 27 co-sponsors and was pulled from the Council’s final meeting of 2025 without a vote. Vision Zero Reimagined commits to hardened daylighting or better at a minimum of 1,000 intersections a year, which is a real commitment and the first of its kind in a plan, but it is not universal daylighting and it does not end the city’s exemption. At 1,000 a year, a city of roughly 40,000 intersections takes decades.
CB6’s record: what the board has voted for since 2010

CB6 positions over the years

Every position Brooklyn Community Board 6 has taken on speed, street design, bike lanes, trucks, transit and enforcement since 2010, what it adds up to, and how it lines up with the plan the city just published.

Sixteen years, %COUNT% votes, one direction of travel. What the board asked for in 2010 and what it asks for now are recognizably the same argument, made with steadily sharper tools.

2010 to 2013, one intersection at a time. %E1% of these votes fall in the first four years, and nearly all of them are a single corner, a single block, a single corral: Grand Army Plaza, Bartel-Pritchard Square, a bike corral outside a shop on 5th Avenue, pedestrian ramps and granite curbs to go with the Prospect Park West median islands. The board asked DOT for a pedestrian safety plan where Atlantic Avenue, Columbia Street and Furman Street meet at Pier 6. It backed the Boerum Hill Neighborhood Slow Zone in 2012, the first time it voted to slow a whole area rather than fix one crossing. Nothing about this era was smooth: the 4th Avenue redesign was disapproved outright in June 2013 and approved with conditions a month later.

2014 to 2019, the corridor years. Vision Zero arrived and the board voted for the state bill that dropped the citywide speed limit to 25 mph, 33 to nothing. From there the unit of work got bigger: the 4th Avenue median reconstruction, protected bike lanes on 4th Avenue with a demand that DOT extend them north to Bergen and Dean, the 7th Avenue lane, the Gowanus Greenway Connector on Hamilton Avenue, ADA plans demanded from NYC Transit for four stations. In March 2018 the board wrote to the Mayor and DOT asking for safety measures along the whole 9th Street corridor.

2020 to today, the rules themselves. The most recent votes are less about a project in front of the board and more about the rules that govern every street: Van Brunt Street out of the truck route network in 2020; opting the city back in to the state law keeping parked cars 20 feet off an intersection in 2023, then hardened daylighting at every intersection in 2025; the Court Street redesign supported with a list of conditions that reads like this plan’s own toolkit, concrete curbs instead of plastic, 15 to 20 mph, leading pedestrian intervals, loading zones; a Gowanus Low Traffic Neighborhood; an end to criminal summonses for delivery cyclists; and in 2026 a district-wide 20 mph slow zone under Sammy’s Law and a call to move parking enforcement out of the NYPD and back to DOT.

Two of those, hardened daylighting and 20 mph zones, are now marquee commitments in the Mayor’s plan. The board got there first.

What the shorthand means
Vision Zero
The city’s commitment, since 2014, to eliminate traffic deaths and severe injuries. Vision Zero Reimagined is the 2026 action plan renewing it, with eight goals and 111 actions across 19 agencies.
Priority Corridor, Priority Intersection
Streets and intersections DOT has singled out for safety work because of their crash history: 199 corridors and 304 intersections citywide.
Daylighting
Keeping the corners of an intersection clear of parked cars so drivers and people crossing can see each other. Hardened daylighting adds something physical, such as a planter, bollard or bike corral, so it cannot be parked on.
LPI
Leading Pedestrian Interval. The walk signal comes on a few seconds before the light turns green for drivers.
Sammy’s Law
The 2024 State law that let the city set speed limits below 25 mph, named for Sammy Cohen Eckstein, killed by a driver on Prospect Park West in 2013. The city is putting 15 mph School Slow Zones at every eligible school and the plan proposes 20 mph zones across whole neighborhoods.
ISA
Intelligent Speed Assistance, a device that reads the speed limit and keeps a vehicle from exceeding it. The Super Speeder program will require it for drivers with 16 or more speed camera violations in a year.
Major Violator
The plan’s term for a driver in a hit and run, or without a valid license, or with three or more prior convictions, a prior suspension or a DWI. Involved in 52 percent of traffic deaths in 2025.
Killed, injured
From the NYPD crash reports on NYC Open Data, crashes with at least one person hurt, placed inside CB6 by the crash coordinates.
About the maps, and why streets and housing go together

Street safety and housing are connected. Building more homes increases density, and density requires better and more environmentally sound ways to move people. That is why pro-housing and safe streets coalitions usually align, and why a bus stop, a bike lane and a rezoning belong in the same conversation.

CB6 has a particular stake in this. Sammy's Law, which gave New York City the authority to lower speed limits below the state minimum, was named for a child killed by a speeding driver in this district. The Court Street protected bike lane, which CB6 voted unanimously to support, drew a lawsuit that a Brooklyn Supreme Court judge dismissed, holding that decisions about how city streets are used are policy questions for elected and accountable officials rather than the courts.
